## Thesis

Stanford University functions less as a school than as a business that selects and trains a small number of students for positions of power in Silicon Valley, and the same institutional incentives that produce that outcome also protect senior figures when their research records contain problems.

## The main ideas

- Stanford’s budget and culture treat students as a product, with a subset identified early for special access to capital and networks.
- Research misconduct allegations against a sitting university president can remain unaddressed for years until a student newspaper reports them.
- Institutions respond to internal criticism first by denial and legal pressure, then by controlled investigation.
- The same social machinery that recruits undergraduates into venture capital circles also shields those already inside it.
- A freshman reporter can force a resignation by documenting what others had already noted but not published.
- After the resignation, the former president raised substantial new capital for a startup, showing that the loss of the office did not end access to resources.

## The product

Theo Baker arrived at Stanford in 2022 as a seventeen-year-old intending to study computer science. He describes the university as having an annual budget larger than that of 116 countries and nearly twice that of Harvard or Yale. In his account, the institution’s output is not primarily graduates but a filtered set of students marked for roles in technology and finance. Some of these students, he writes, receive early offers of funding before they have a company, invitations to private events, and attention from existing investors.

He places this selection process inside the same environment that hosts ordinary undergraduate life. The book records both the formal curriculum and the parallel set of activities—hackathons, clubs with substantial budgets, and informal recruiting—that operate alongside it. Baker presents the parallel track as the more decisive one for the students it reaches.

The mechanism is that resources concentrate on the small number of students judged likely to produce large outcomes, while the formal academic structure continues for everyone else. The concentration is visible in funding, access, and social attention rather than in official policy statements.

Implication: when evaluating an elite institution’s output, measure the distribution of capital and attention across its students, not the average experience.

## The tip

Baker joined The Stanford Daily shortly after arriving, initially to honor a grandfather who had worked on a college paper. His first reporting concerned campus social restrictions. A later tip directed him to comments on PubPeer, a site where scientists discuss published papers, noting irregularities in images from papers co-authored by Marc Tessier-Lavigne, then Stanford’s president and a neuroscientist who had previously worked at Genentech.

Baker took the comments to forensic image analysts, including Elisabeth Bik, who examined the figures and identified alterations that, in her assessment, suggested an intent to mislead. The first Daily story on the matter appeared in November 2022. Within a day the university’s board announced its own investigation. Baker continued reporting, eventually covering roughly a dozen papers, including a 2009 Nature paper on Alzheimer’s research that former Genentech colleagues told him had been the subject of an internal review finding fabricated data.

The mechanism is that specialized scientific discussion sites can contain unresolved questions about high-profile papers for years; a reporter who treats those questions as leads and obtains independent expert review can convert them into a published record.

Implication: published scientific claims that have not been independently re-examined remain open to later scrutiny, regardless of the author’s current position.

## The response

After the first story, Baker received legal correspondence from an attorney representing Tessier-Lavigne stating that the allegations were completely and utterly false. Tessier-Lavigne emailed faculty that the reporting contained falsehoods. Baker also reported that a member of the board committee overseeing the investigation held an $18 million investment in Denali Therapeutics, a company Tessier-Lavigne had co-founded; that trustee later stepped aside.

The university’s eventual panel report, released in 2023, found no evidence that Tessier-Lavigne had personally manipulated data. It did find that he had failed to correct mistakes in the scientific record on multiple occasions after being made aware of problems, and that labs he supervised had produced papers containing manipulated images. Tessier-Lavigne resigned as president in July 2023. Several papers were later retracted or corrected. Months afterward he raised approximately $1 billion for a new company.

The mechanism is that an institution under scrutiny can simultaneously open a formal process, limit the scope of that process, and maintain public statements of confidence in the subject while the process runs. The formal finding can then address a narrower question than the one the original reporting raised.

Implication: when an organization investigates one of its own leaders, the terms of the investigation and the composition of the investigators are part of the record, not only the final conclusion.

## The parallel track

Baker describes a second set of activities at Stanford that run alongside classes. These include a student organization whose end-of-year event he helped fund with university money, resulting in a yacht rental, and informal recruiting in which venture capitalists identify undergraduates before those students have formed companies. He records being approached for a class or group called “How to Rule the World,” presented as a selective opportunity for students judged to have high agency.

He also reports on a student who lived in university housing for an extended period without being enrolled, an episode the administration knew about and did not promptly resolve. Baker treats these as instances of the same pattern: rules applied unevenly once a person is inside a preferred category.

The mechanism is that access to capital and to institutional tolerance can be granted on the basis of perceived future value rather than on current compliance with stated rules. Once granted, that access is difficult to withdraw without also withdrawing the expected return.

Implication: in environments where a small number of participants are expected to generate outsized returns, the enforcement of ordinary rules becomes selective.

## The cost

Baker records that the reporting consumed most of his time during the academic year. He describes falling behind in coursework, the end of a relationship, and a medical emergency at a student event after he took oxycodone. He also notes that some observers attributed his access to sources to his parents’ positions in journalism rather than to the work itself. The book states that the work consisted of repeated interviews, document review, and follow-up with sources who required anonymity.

The mechanism is that sustained investigation of a sitting institutional leader requires resources the investigator does not control—time, legal support, and continued access to the institution—while the subject of the investigation retains those resources by default.

Implication: the feasibility of internal reporting depends on whether the reporter can absorb the opportunity cost for longer than the institution can delay a response.

## Limits

- The university’s panel concluded that Tessier-Lavigne did not personally manipulate data; the book’s strongest documented claim is failure to correct known problems, not personal fabrication.
- Some reviewers have argued that the book’s account of student culture generalizes from a small number of highly visible cases and understates the experience of students outside the recruiting track.
- The book is a memoir as well as a report; passages about Baker’s personal life and family are not independently documented in the same way as the research allegations.
