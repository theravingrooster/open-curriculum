# The Turner Diaries
William Luther Pierce (as Andrew Macdonald) (1978)

A novel written by the founder of a neo-Nazi organization that depicts a violent racial revolution in the United States and has been cited by multiple domestic terrorists as an influence.

Key concepts: Organization · System · race war

## Overview

The book is a work of fiction first serialized in the newspaper of the National Alliance and published in book form in 1978. Pierce, a physicist by training who later led the National Alliance, wrote it under a pseudonym. It is presented as the recovered diary of a character named Earl Turner who participates in an underground group called the Organization. The narrative follows the group's campaign against the federal government and against non-white populations. The novel ends with a global racial conflict and the elimination of groups the author regards as enemies.

The work is explicitly racist and antisemitic. It has been described by the Southern Poverty Law Center and others as a foundational text for segments of the white-nationalist movement in the late twentieth century. Its practical influence is documented: members of the Order (a 1980s white-supremacist group) took their name and operational ideas from the book; Timothy McVeigh, convicted of the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing, possessed and repeatedly read a copy. The novel has therefore functioned both as propaganda and as a reference point for people who later committed real violence.

Because the book is a fictional blueprint rather than a historical account or a work of political philosophy with falsifiable claims, it does not supply transferable knowledge in the sense used by the rest of this curriculum. Its value, if any, is historical: it shows one form that late-twentieth-century American racial radicalism took and the specific text that several later terrorists treated as inspiration. Readers who want primary material on the period are better served by contemporaneous reporting, court records from the relevant cases, and scholarly histories of the extreme right.

## Limits

The novel contains graphic depictions of mass violence and genocide. It has no evidentiary status. Its circulation has been restricted at various times precisely because of its documented role in inspiring terrorism. It is included here only as a primary document of an extremist subculture, not as a source of ideas to be used.
