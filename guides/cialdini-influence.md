## Thesis

People comply with requests largely through a small number of automatic shortcuts, and anyone who knows how to trigger those shortcuts can get a yes without the target ever weighing the request on its merits.

## The main ideas

- Most decisions run on fixed responses to single cues, which are efficient and exploitable.
- Reciprocity: people feel bound to repay what they are given, including concessions.
- Commitment and consistency: once people take a stand, they bend later choices to match it.
- Social proof: when uncertain, people do what similar others are doing.
- Liking: people say yes to those they like, and liking can be manufactured.
- Authority: people defer to symbols of expertise, often without checking the expertise.
- Scarcity: things seem more valuable as they become harder to get. The 2021 edition adds a seventh principle, unity: people favour those they see as one of us.

## Click, whirr

Cialdini opens with animal behaviour. A mother turkey mothers her chicks almost entirely in response to one cue, the "cheep-cheep" sound they make. In an experiment, a stuffed polecat, the turkey's natural enemy, fitted with a recorder playing that sound was gathered in and mothered; with the recorder off, the turkey attacked it. The sound triggered a fixed sequence of behaviour, which Cialdini calls "click, whirr," like a tape starting.

People run similar tapes, for the same reason: the world is too complex to evaluate every decision, so we respond to a single feature that is usually a reliable guide. Ellen Langer's study at a copying machine shows how thin the cue can be. Asking to cut in line with a real reason ("because I'm in a rush") got 94% compliance; with no reason, 60%; with an empty reason ("because I have to make some copies"), 93%. The word "because" did most of the work. A second case: a jewellery shop in Arizona could not sell a case of turquoise until the owner's note to price it "x ½" was misread as "x 2." At double the price it sold out, because shoppers used price as a shortcut for quality.

Implication: the shortcuts are usually right, which is what makes them exploitable. When a decision matters, notice when a single cue, a reason, a price, a title, is standing in for an actual evaluation.

## Reciprocity

People feel obliged to repay what another person has given them, even when the gift was unwanted and the giver is disliked. The rule is universal because it lets societies trade and cooperate: it assures the giver that giving is not a loss. Cialdini's cases: Hare Krishna members pressed a flower on travellers at airports as "a gift," then asked for a donation, and the unrequested gift made refusal uncomfortable. In 1985, famine-stricken Ethiopia sent $5,000 in aid to Mexico after the Mexico City earthquake, repaying aid Mexico had sent in 1935, when Italy invaded Ethiopia.

The rule also covers concessions, which produces the rejection-then-retreat technique. Cialdini's team asked students to spend two hours a week for at least two years as counsellors to juvenile delinquents. Everyone refused. The team then asked for a smaller favour: chaperoning a group of delinquents on a day trip to the zoo. Fifty percent agreed, against 17% of students who were asked about the zoo trip directly. The retreat from the large request felt like a concession that had to be matched.

Implication: an unsolicited gift or a sudden concession is a request in disguise. Judge the offer on its merits, and treat a free sample or a lowered price as a tactic, not a favour to be repaid.

## Commitment and consistency

Once people commit to a position, especially actively, publicly and with effort, they feel pressure to behave consistently with it, and they generate new reasons to support it. Consistency is valued because it is usually a sign of reliability, and it saves the effort of rethinking every decision.

Cialdini's clearest case is the treatment of American prisoners in Chinese camps during the Korean War. Rather than use brutality, captors asked for small statements, such as agreeing that the United States was not perfect, then asked prisoners to write out and elaborate them, and entered essays in political contests with small prizes. Having written the statements, many prisoners came to believe them. In a Palo Alto study by Freedman and Fraser, homeowners were asked to allow a large, badly lettered "Drive Carefully" sign on their front lawn. Only 17% agreed. Among those who had first agreed to a three-inch "Be a safe driver" sign in their window, 76% agreed to the large one.

Implication: be wary of small first requests, and of written or public commitments, because they bind later choices. Ask of a commitment you are defending, "knowing what I know now, would I make it again?"

## Social proof

When people are unsure what to do, they look at what others like them are doing and treat it as correct. The shortcut is usually sound, since many people are rarely all wrong, but it fails under uncertainty, when everyone is looking at everyone else. Canned laughter makes audiences laugh longer and rate material funnier, even though they know it is fake.

Cialdini's heaviest cases are about imitation under uncertainty. David Phillips found that front-page stories about suicides were followed by increases in suicides, and by increases in fatal car and plane crashes. The mass deaths at Jonestown in 1978, in which more than 900 members of the Peoples Temple died, happened in an isolated jungle settlement where members had no one to compare themselves with but each other. Cialdini also tells the 1964 murder of Kitty Genovese in New York, reported as watched by 38 witnesses who did nothing, as an illustration of pluralistic ignorance: each bystander looking to the others, seeing no one act, and concluding there was no emergency.

Implication: in an emergency, single out one person and give a direct instruction. When a crowd's behaviour is your evidence, ask whether the crowd knows more than you do or is just as uncertain.

## Liking

People say yes more readily to those they like, and liking can be produced deliberately through similarity, compliments, contact and association. Joe Girard, listed by Guinness as the world's greatest car salesman, sent each of his more than 13,000 former customers a card every month that read "I like you." The Tupperware party, which Cialdini calls the quintessential American compliance setting, works because the request comes from a friend, the hostess, who gets a share of the sales, so buyers are pleasing a friend as much as buying a product.

The mechanism is that liking is a quick proxy for trust, and it transfers to whatever the liked person is attached to. That is why sellers stress shared backgrounds, why compliments work even when people know they are flattery, and why brands pay for association with things people already like.

Implication: when you notice you like a salesperson or negotiator unusually quickly, separate your feelings about the person from your judgment of the deal.

## Authority

People defer to authority, and to symbols of it such as titles, uniforms and expensive clothes, often without checking whether the authority is real or relevant. Obedience is trained from childhood and is usually useful, since experts usually know more.

Stanley Milgram's experiments are Cialdini's central evidence: about two-thirds of ordinary participants continued to the maximum 450-volt shock on a supposed learner, because a man in a lab coat told them to. The hospital study by Hofling and colleagues is closer to everyday life. A caller claiming to be an unknown doctor phoned ward nurses and ordered them to give an excessive dose of an unauthorized drug, "Astroten." Of 22 nurses, 21 were stopped on the way to the patient's bed, about to comply.

Implication: when someone invokes authority, ask two questions: is this person actually an expert, and how truthful are they likely to be here. A title or uniform answers neither.

## Scarcity

Things seem more valuable as they become less available, and the loss of a freedom makes people want it more. Scarcity works because hard-to-get things usually are better, and because losing options provokes reactance, an urge to regain them.

The cookie study by Worchel and colleagues isolates the effect. People rated identical cookies as more desirable when they came from a jar holding two than from a jar holding ten. They rated them higher still when the jar had just gone from ten to two, and highest of all when the drop was blamed on high demand from others. Newly scarce things and things competed for are valued most. Cialdini applies the same logic to "limited number" and "deadline" tactics and to parental interference in romance, citing research in which couples reported more love as parental opposition increased.

Implication: when you feel a rush to act before something runs out, ask whether you want the thing for its use or for its scarcity. The cookies tasted no better from the smaller jar.

## Limits

- The "38 witnesses" account of the Genovese murder was exaggerated. A 2007 review by Manning, Levine and Collins found no evidence that 38 people watched and did nothing, and in 2016 the New York Times wrote that its original story had grossly exaggerated the number of witnesses and what they perceived.
- The hospital obedience study did not hold up under realistic conditions. Rank and Jacobson (1977) repeated it with a known drug, a real doctor's name and nurses free to consult colleagues; only 2 of 18 complied.
- Some of the book's evidence has not replicated. A 2014 study of 396 people by Sinclair, Hood and Wright found that disapproval from friends and parents went with worse relationships, not the "Romeo and Juliet" effect. The rejection-then-retreat technique, by contrast, was confirmed in a direct replication in 2021.
