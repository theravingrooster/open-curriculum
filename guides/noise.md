## Thesis

Professional judgments that should be identical vary far more than anyone expects, this random scatter, which the authors call noise, causes as much error as bias, and it can be measured and reduced by structuring how judgments are made.

## The main ideas

- Error has two components: bias, a shared lean in one direction, and noise, scatter around the average. Each adds to total error.
- Noise can be measured without knowing the right answer, by comparing different people's judgments of the same case.
- Organizations badly underestimate their own noise. A noise audit typically finds several times what executives expect.
- Noise comes from differences in each judge's average level, from each judge's idiosyncratic reaction to particular cases, and from the judge's state on a given day.
- Human judgment predicts outcomes weakly, and simple rules usually predict better, including models built from the judge's own decisions.
- Decision hygiene reduces noise: independent judgments, aggregated; decisions broken into separate assessments; overall impressions delayed.

## Bias and noise

The authors illustrate the two kinds of error with a shooting range. Four teams of five each fire at a target. Team A's shots cluster tightly on the bull's-eye. Team B's cluster tightly, but below and to the left: that is bias. Team C's are scattered around the center with no consistent direction: that is noise. Team D's are both scattered and off center.

The back of the target shows something important. Looking only at the pattern of holes, without seeing the bull's-eye, one can tell that teams C and D are noisy, but not whether any team is biased. Noise can be measured without knowing the true value, which is why it can be studied in judgments such as sentences, diagnoses or valuations where the right answer is never known.

The authors measure total error as mean squared error and show that it decomposes exactly: mean squared error equals bias squared plus noise squared. A reduction in noise therefore improves accuracy by the same amount as an equal reduction in bias. Because bias is easier to see and to tell stories about, organizations attend to it and neglect noise.

Implication: the variability in a group's judgments of the same case is error, even when the average is right. Reducing it is as valuable as correcting a known bias.

## The noise audit

A noise audit gives several professionals the same realistic cases and measures how far their judgments differ. The authors ran one at a large insurance company. Underwriters priced identical policies, and claims adjusters valued identical claims.

Before seeing results, executives were asked how much two randomly chosen professionals would differ, as a percentage of their average. Most expected about 10 percent. The median difference among underwriters was 55 percent. Among claims adjusters it was 43 percent.

The mechanism is that professionals rarely see each other's judgments of the same case. Each underwriter works alone, gets little feedback on whether a premium was right, and assumes colleagues would reach roughly the same figure. Since no one compares, the scatter stays invisible, and its cost, in policies priced too low or business lost by pricing too high, is never attributed to it.

Implication: an organization that relies on expert judgment should run its own audit before assuming its experts agree. The size of the disagreement is an empirical question, and the answer is usually large.

## Noise in the courts

The authors' longest case is criminal sentencing. In 1974 the federal judge Marvin Frankel helped run a study in which 50 federal judges sentenced the same hypothetical defendants. Absence of consensus was the norm. A heroin dealer might receive 1 year or 10 depending on the judge, and a bank robber 5 years or 18. Another study, with 208 federal judges and 16 cases, found unanimous agreement on whether to impose a prison term in only 3 of the 16.

Frankel's campaign led to the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 and to sentencing guidelines that sharply reduced disparity between judges. Judges objected that the guidelines were mechanical and ignored the particulars of cases. In 2005 the Supreme Court's *Booker* decision made the guidelines advisory, and disparity between judges increased again.

The noise extends beyond sentencing. In one Miami courthouse, one immigration judge granted asylum to 88 percent of applicants and another to 5 percent. The outcome for an applicant depended heavily on which judge they drew.

Implication: where the same rules are applied by many decision makers to similar cases, assume large disparity until it is measured, and judge any proposed reform by whether it reduces it.

## Level, pattern and occasion noise

The authors split system noise, the variability across a set of judges, into parts. Level noise is the difference in judges' average severity: some judges are harsher than others across all cases. Pattern noise is a judge's idiosyncratic reaction to particular cases, harsher than average for some kinds of defendant and more lenient for others. Pattern noise includes a stable part, a lasting feature of the judge, and occasion noise, which varies with the moment. Stable pattern noise is usually the largest component.

Occasion noise is the most surprising, because it means the same person judges the same case differently at different times. The book's evidence: juvenile court judges in Louisiana gave longer sentences in the week after an unexpected loss by the Louisiana State University football team. Immigration judges granted asylum less often on hot days. Doctors prescribe opioids more often toward the end of the day. Fingerprint examiners shown the same prints months apart sometimes reached different conclusions, and were pushed toward a match by irrelevant context such as being told the suspect had confessed. Wine judges tasting the same wine twice gave it the same score only about 18 percent of the time.

Implication: a single judgment by a single person is a sample, not a measurement. For important cases, get more than one, at different times or from different people.

## Groups amplify noise

Groups are often expected to cancel individual errors. The authors argue that discussion can instead make outcomes noisier, because early contributions shape everything that follows. Their evidence is Matthew Salganik's MusicLab experiment, in which participants could listen to and download 72 songs by unknown bands. In some versions they could see how often each song had been downloaded. Separate groups, starting from the same songs, produced very different hits, because early downloads, not quality, drove later ones.

Two mechanisms produce this. In an informational cascade, people who speak later defer to those who spoke earlier, so the first opinions in a meeting carry more weight than their merits warrant. In group polarization, deliberation pushes a group toward a more extreme version of the position its members initially leaned toward. Both make a group's conclusion depend on accidents of who spoke first and how confidently.

Implication: before a group discusses a judgment, have each member record their own estimate independently. Aggregate those first, then discuss.

## Objective ignorance and simple models

The authors argue that much of what makes outcomes hard to predict is objective ignorance: information that no one could have at the time of the judgment. Experts underestimate it, which the authors call the illusion of validity.

They measure predictive accuracy with percent concordant: the chance that, of two cases, the one ranked higher by the judgment also scores higher on the outcome. Fifty percent means no relationship. Human judges in personnel selection average a correlation of .28 with later performance, a percent concordant of 59 percent, while executives typically guess 75 to 85.

Simple models do better. In Lewis Goldberg's work, a linear model built to imitate a judge's own past decisions predicted outcomes better than the judge, because it applied the judge's policy without the judge's noise. Robyn Dawes found that improper linear models, which weight all relevant variables equally, perform about as well as optimally weighted regression, and models with random weights also beat human judges.

Implication: where a decision recurs and outcomes can be checked, compare human judgment against a simple rule using the same inputs. If the rule does as well, use it, or use it as a check.

## Decision hygiene

The authors call noise-reduction methods decision hygiene: procedures that prevent errors without anyone knowing which errors they prevented. The first is aggregating independent judgments. Even one person can do this partially. In the work of Edward Vul and Harold Pashler, a second guess from the same person, averaged with the first, gave about a tenth of the benefit of a second person's opinion, rising to about a third when the second guess came three weeks later.

The second is structure. The authors' mediating assessments protocol breaks a decision into separate assessments of predefined dimensions, has each assessed independently, and delays the overall judgment until all are complete. It descends from the interview system Kahneman designed for the Israeli army in 1956, in which interviewers rated recruits on separate traits instead of forming an overall impression. Structured interviews follow the same logic. A third practice is sequencing information so that judges see only what they need, keeping irrelevant facts, such as a suspect's confession, from reaching a fingerprint examiner.

Implication: for any repeated high-stakes judgment, define the dimensions in advance, assess them separately and independently, and form the overall view last.

## Limits

- The economist John Kay argued that most real decisions have no identifiable correct answer, even afterwards, and that differences of judgment can be valuable, because they advance collective knowledge under radical uncertainty. The book acknowledges costs of noise reduction in its section on optimal noise, but treats variability chiefly as error.
- The economist Rachael Meager and the statistician Andrew Gelman criticized the book's chapter 12 for repeatedly treating the absence of correlation as evidence of the absence of causation. Meager's counterexample: a driver presses the accelerator harder uphill to keep speed constant, so pressure and speed are uncorrelated even though one causes the other.
- Andrew Gelman and Gaurav Sood, reviewing the book in *Chance* in 2024, criticized it for citing very small studies without qualification. Their example is the software estimation study behind the claim that the same developers' estimates of the same task varied by 71 percent on different days, which had seven participants.
