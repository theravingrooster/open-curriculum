## Thesis

Through three practices, controlled deep breathing, gradual exposure to cold and committed focus of the mind, people can consciously influence bodily systems usually considered automatic, including the stress response and inflammation, and so improve their health and resilience.

Strength of evidence: mixed. Small trials show the breathing raises adrenaline and can dampen the inflammatory response in a laboratory setting; claims that the method treats disease go well beyond the evidence, and the breathing can cause fainting, which has proved fatal in water.

## The main ideas

- The method rests on three pillars: breathing, cold and the power of the mind.
- The breathing exercise is rounds of 30 to 40 deep breaths, each followed by a breath hold after exhaling.
- Cold exposure is built up gradually, starting by ending a warm shower with 30 seconds of cold water.
- Commitment, the mental pillar, is what turns the practices into lasting change.
- Hof argues that the method lets people influence the immune and stress responses, citing experiments at Radboud University.
- The breathing must never be done in or near water, or while driving.

## The Iceman's origins

Wim Hof, a Dutchman known as the Iceman, opens with how the method began. On a winter Sunday he saw thin ice on the water in a park, felt drawn to it, undressed and went in. He returned to the cold water daily, and it became a ritual.

The cold took on a second meaning after personal tragedy. Hof met Olaya, from the Basque Country, during his years living in squats. She suffered from depression and died by suicide. The chapter devoted to her describes how he found stillness in cold water during his grief.

The book's chapters follow the method's development from there: from "The Birth of the Iceman" through chapters on the cold, the breath and the mind, to its applications in "WHM for Health" and "WHM for Performance," the scientific case in "The Truth Is on Our Side," and a final, more spiritual section.

The mechanism Hof proposes is that deliberate exposure to discomfort, practiced daily, trains a person's control over responses that otherwise run automatically, and that the same control that let him endure cold helped him endure loss.

Implication: the method is presented as a daily discipline, not a one-time technique; its claimed benefits depend on repetition.

## Breath

The breathing exercise is the center of the method. Sitting or lying down, the practitioner takes 30 to 40 deep breaths, filling the belly, then the chest, then the head, and letting each breath out without force. After the last exhale, they hold the breath for as long as they can without forcing it. They then take a full breath in and hold it for about 10 to 15 seconds before releasing. That is one round; Hof recommends three or four, ideally before breakfast on an empty stomach, taking about 20 minutes.

Hof argues that deliberate deep breathing changes the body's chemistry and focuses the mind.

The mechanism is hyperventilation. Rapid deep breathing lowers carbon dioxide in the blood, which allows the long breath hold that follows, and it triggers a surge of adrenaline. That adrenaline, Hof argues, is how the method influences the stress and immune responses.

The same mechanism is the source of its main danger: lowered carbon dioxide removes the urge to breathe, and a practitioner can lose consciousness during the hold without warning.

Implication: practice the breathing only sitting or lying down in a safe place, never in or near water, in a bath, or while driving.

## Cold

Cold exposure, Hof argues, trains the cardiovascular system and the body's response to stress. The book's chapter title gives the program in brief: a cold shower a day keeps the doctor away.

The program is gradual. The practitioner takes a normal warm shower and ends it with cold water, at least five days a week. In the first week the cold lasts 30 seconds, in the second a minute, in the third a minute and a half, and in the fourth two minutes. Those who find 30 seconds too much can start at 15.

The mechanism Hof describes is adaptation: the body's shock response to cold diminishes with repeated exposure, and the practitioner learns to stay calm in it. The gradual increase is what makes the adaptation possible without overwhelming the body.

Implication: start small and increase slowly. The value of the practice lies in regular, tolerable exposure, not in dramatic plunges.

## The mind

The third pillar is the mind, which Hof frames as commitment. Breathing and cold are tools, but what makes them work, he argues, is the decision to practice every day and the focus brought to each session.

He presents commitment as the bridge between a technique and a change in a person's life. The chapter "The Power of the Mind" argues that people underestimate their capacity to influence their own physiology, and that belief and focus are part of the method, not an extra.

The book's later chapters, including "Freeing Our Ancestral Burden," "Beyond the Five Senses" and "Into the Light Within," extend this into a spiritual account of the method, in which practice opens people to experiences beyond the ordinary.

The mechanism is habit. A practice continued daily for months has effects that one attempted occasionally does not.

Implication: decide in advance when and how you will practice, and judge the method only after weeks of regular practice, not after a single session.

## The science

The book's case that the method works rests on experiments at Radboud University in the Netherlands. In the first, Hof himself was injected with endotoxin, a component of bacteria that normally provokes a strong inflammatory reaction with flu-like symptoms, and, using his techniques, he showed a much weaker response. The book presents this as unprecedented, and as evidence that the immune response, long considered involuntary, can be influenced.

The chapter "The Truth Is on Our Side" uses this and related research to argue that the method's effects are measurable and not merely subjective. From the same basis, the book presents the method as a way to fight disease, pain and trauma.

The mechanism the research points to is the adrenaline surge produced by the breathing. Adrenaline raises the body's production of anti-inflammatory signals and dampens the inflammatory ones.

Implication: the laboratory findings support a specific, short-term effect on inflammation. They do not establish that the method treats any disease, and claims that it does should be judged against controlled evidence.

## Limits

- The trial evidence is small. A 2024 systematic review in *PLOS ONE* by Almahayni and Hammond found eight trials, with 15 to 48 participants each and most of them men, seven of them at high risk of bias. It found possible anti-inflammatory effects, mixed effects on exercise performance, and called for larger and more rigorous trials.
- The active ingredient may be ordinary hyperventilation. A 2022 study by Zwaag and colleagues found that the breathing raised adrenaline regardless of who taught it or how long participants had trained, while cold exposure alone did not change the inflammatory response.
- The breathing has been linked to drownings. A Dutch newspaper, *Het Parool*, reported four drownings of practitioners in 2015 and 2016. In 2022, a 17-year-old in California drowned in a pool after doing the breathing; her family sued Hof, and a court found him not liable in 2024.
