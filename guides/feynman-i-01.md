## Thesis

All things are made of atoms in perpetual motion, attracting when a little distance apart and repelling when squeezed together, and this single idea explains why solids hold their shape, why liquids flow, why gases press on their containers, and why heat is motion.

## The main ideas

- The atomic hypothesis is the sentence that would carry the most scientific information if all other knowledge were lost.
- Atoms are one or two angstroms across; a drop of water contains on the order of 10²³ molecules.
- The perpetual motion of atoms is the source of pressure, temperature, and chemical change.
- Experiment is the sole judge of scientific truth, but the laws themselves must be guessed by imagination and then tested.
- From the atomic picture follow evaporation, the distinction between solids, liquids, and gases, and the nature of heat.

## Atoms

Feynman begins with a thought experiment: all scientific knowledge is destroyed and only one sentence can be passed on. The sentence that carries the most information in the fewest words is the atomic hypothesis. All things are made of atoms, little particles that move around in perpetual motion, attracting each other when they are a little distance apart, but repelling upon being squeezed into one another.

He then shows what follows. In a solid the atoms are locked in place by the attractions, vibrating but not free to move past one another. In a liquid they stay close but can slide. In a gas they are far apart and fly freely until they hit something. Heat is the energy of this motion. Evaporation occurs when a few molecules at the surface happen to have enough speed to escape the attractions of their neighbors.

The mechanism is that a single correct picture of the underlying particles accounts for an enormous range of everyday phenomena that otherwise look unrelated. Once the picture is accepted, pressure, temperature, phase changes, and chemical reactions become consequences rather than separate facts.

Implication: when trying to understand a complex subject, look for the one underlying idea from which the most else follows.

## The method

The same chapter states the method. The test of all knowledge is experiment. Experiment is the sole judge of scientific truth, but it does not hand over the laws. Those must be guessed, with imagination, and then tested against further experiment. The atomic hypothesis itself was a guess that survived.

Implication: treat a proposed law as a guess that has not yet been contradicted by experiment, not as a revealed truth.
