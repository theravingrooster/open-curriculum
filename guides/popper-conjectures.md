## Thesis

Knowledge grows not by accumulating observations that confirm a theory but by proposing bold conjectures and trying to refute them, so a theory is scientific only if some possible observation could show it false, and progress in science and politics alike comes from detecting and correcting errors.

## The main ideas

- A theory is scientific if it can be refuted. A theory that every possible event confirms is not strong but empty.
- Confirmations count only when they come from risky predictions, ones that could have failed.
- Rescuing a refuted theory by reinterpreting it or the evidence lowers its scientific status.
- Induction, inferring theories from many observations, is a myth. Theories come first, as guesses, and observation tests them.
- No source of knowledge is authoritative. The question is not where an idea came from but how its errors can be found.
- Rational tradition is a tradition of criticism, which began with the early Greek philosophers.
- In politics, fight concrete evils piece by piece. Utopian blueprints tend to produce violence.

## The problem of demarcation

The book's title essay, a lecture given at Cambridge in 1953, describes the question Popper began asking in Vienna in 1919: when should a theory be ranked as scientific? He contrasted Einstein's theory of relativity with three theories then influential in Vienna: Marx's theory of history, Freud's psychoanalysis and Alfred Adler's individual psychology.

What struck him about the three was their explanatory power. Their followers saw confirmations everywhere; whatever happened, the theory could explain it. Popper's illustration is a meeting with Adler in 1919. He described a case to Adler that did not seem particularly Adlerian, and Adler analyzed it at once in terms of feelings of inferiority, without having seen the child. Asked how he could be so sure, Adler answered, "Because of my thousandfold experience." Popper replied that with this new case, his experience had presumably become thousand-and-one-fold.

The point is that each earlier case had itself been interpreted in the light of the theory and then counted as a confirmation. A theory that can interpret every case in its own terms is confirmed by every case, which means that no case tests it.

Implication: when a theory or an expert explains every outcome, including opposite ones, treat that as a weakness. Ask what result would show it wrong; if there is none, it is not telling you anything about the world.

## Risky predictions

Einstein's theory differed in the risk it took. It predicted that light from distant stars passing near the sun would be bent by its gravity, so stars near the sun would appear shifted from their usual positions. In 1919 Eddington's eclipse expedition photographed the stars near the darkened sun and found the predicted shift. What impressed Popper was the risk: if the shift had not been observed, the theory would have been refuted.

From the contrast Popper drew his conclusions. Confirmations should count only when they result from risky predictions, ones that, without the theory, we would have expected to fail. Every good scientific theory is a prohibition: it forbids certain things to happen, and the more it forbids, the better it is. "A theory which is not refutable by any conceivable event is non-scientific." Irrefutability is not a virtue of a theory, as people often think, but a vice. Every genuine test of a theory is an attempt to falsify it, and the criterion of a theory's scientific status is its falsifiability, or testability.

Implication: to test a plan, a model or a belief, state in advance what outcome would count against it. A prediction that could not have failed provides no evidence.

## The conventionalist stratagem

A testable theory can be made untestable after it fails. Popper's case is Marxism. In its early forms, Marx's theory of history made testable predictions, and those predictions were falsified. Instead of accepting the refutations, Marx's followers reinterpreted both the theory and the evidence to make them agree.

Popper's judgment is that this rescued the theory from refutation only at the price of destroying, or at least lowering, its scientific status. He calls such a move a conventionalist twist or conventionalist stratagem: introducing an auxiliary assumption, or reinterpreting the theory, so that a failed prediction no longer counts against it. Some theories, such as psychoanalysis, were never testable in the first place. Marxism had been testable and was made untestable by its defenders.

The mechanism is that there is always some way to explain away a failure. Each such adjustment protects the theory and reduces what it says, until it forbids nothing.

Implication: when a forecast or strategy fails and its defenders explain why the failure does not count, check whether the revised version still forbids anything. A theory that survives every failure by being reinterpreted is no longer being tested.

## Induction is a myth

Popper rejects the view that science starts from observations and generalizes from them. In his words, "Induction, i.e. inference based on many observations, is a myth." It is not how people think, nor how science proceeds. Knowledge grows instead by unjustified anticipations, guesses and conjectures, which are then controlled by criticism, including attempts at refutation.

His demonstration is a lecture he gave to physics students in Vienna. He told them to take pencil and paper, observe carefully and write down what they had observed. They asked what they were supposed to observe. Popper's point is that a bare instruction to observe is absurd: observation always needs a chosen object, a definite task, an interest, a point of view, a problem. Observations are selected and interpreted by expectations or theories that come before them.

The mechanism is that a theory cannot be built from observations, because observations cannot be made without a theory to direct them. The theory comes first, as a guess, and observation's job is to test it.

Implication: begin an inquiry with an explicit hypothesis and design observations to test it. Gathering data without a question to answer produces material that cannot settle anything.

## No ultimate sources of knowledge

In "On the Sources of Knowledge and of Ignorance," Popper argues that the traditional question of epistemology, what are the best sources of knowledge, has no good answer. Neither observation nor reason, tradition nor authority, is an ideal source. All of them can lead us into error.

He proposes replacing the question with another: how can we hope to detect and eliminate error? His answer is by criticizing the theories and guesses of others and, if we can train ourselves to do it, our own. The origin of an idea, whether a scientist, an expert or a sacred text, is no guarantee of it; what matters is whether it survives criticism. "Our knowledge can only be finite, while our ignorance must necessarily be infinite." In that infinite ignorance, Popper adds, we are all equal.

The mechanism is that demanding authoritative sources leads to dogmatism, because any source treated as authoritative is shielded from criticism. Treating every source as fallible keeps every claim open to correction.

Implication: evaluate a claim by how it holds up against attempts to refute it, not by who made it. Build institutions and teams in which criticism of any idea, including the leader's, is expected.

## The tradition of criticism

Popper locates the beginning of rational thought in the Ionian philosophers of ancient Greece. In "Back to the Presocratics," he argues that their school started something new: a tradition of critical discussion. Thales proposed a theory of the world; his pupil Anaximander criticized it and proposed another; Anaximander's pupil Anaximenes criticized his in turn. Each pupil was free to criticize the teacher. Popper contrasts this with the Pythagorean school, which preserved its founder's doctrines unchanged.

The ideas were bold. Anaximander held that the earth hangs unsupported in space, staying where it is because it is equidistant from everything, a theory based on argument from symmetry rather than observation. Popper calls it one of the boldest, most revolutionary and most portentous ideas in the history of human thought.

In "Towards a Rational Theory of Tradition," Popper adds that criticism does not mean rejecting tradition. We have to know and understand a tradition before we can criticize it, and progress comes from correcting traditions, not from starting over.

Implication: judge a school of thought, a profession or an organization by whether it allows its founding ideas to be criticized. One that preserves them unchanged has stopped learning.

## Piecemeal reform

In "Utopia and Violence," a 1947 address, Popper applies the same view to politics. The Utopian method chooses an ideal state of society and makes it the aim of all political action. Popper argues that this method tends to produce violence: ideals cannot be tested or agreed on rationally, so disagreement about them can only be settled by force, and the blueprint justifies whatever means it requires.

His alternative is to work against identifiable evils, which people can agree on and whose remedies can be tested: "Work for the elimination of concrete evils rather than for the realization of abstract goods." He adds that governments should not aim to establish happiness by political means.

The mechanism is the same as in science. A specific reform makes a testable claim: if it fails, the failure is visible and the reform can be corrected. A Utopian plan admits no test short of its completion, and its supporters can explain away every failure on the way.

Implication: prefer policies aimed at a measurable harm, introduced in steps that can be evaluated and reversed, over comprehensive plans justified by the society they promise.

## Limits

- Single hypotheses cannot be falsified in isolation, only together with auxiliary assumptions, which is the Duhem–Quine problem. When the orbit of Uranus contradicted Newton's theory, astronomers did not reject the theory; they posited an unseen planet, and Neptune was found in 1846. Imre Lakatos built on such cases a methodology of research programmes, judged as progressive or degenerating over time, not refuted by single tests.
- Thomas Kuhn argued, in a paper given at a 1965 London colloquium chaired by Popper, that most science is normal science, puzzle-solving within an accepted paradigm, not continual attempts at refutation.
- Popper's definition of verisimilitude, closeness to the truth, defined as a theory's true consequences minus its false ones, failed. In 1974 David Miller and Pavel Tichý showed independently that under it, no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other.
