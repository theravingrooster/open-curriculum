## Thesis

Snapchat grew from a Stanford fraternity project into a public company worth billions because its young founder, Evan Spiegel, trusted his instinct for what his own generation wanted, ephemeral and private communication, and refused to sell when Facebook offered billions, a conviction that also produced a bitter split with a cofounder, a secretive company and a product that bigger rivals could copy.

## The main ideas

- A product built around a new behavior, sharing that disappears, can find an audience that established companies miss.
- Young users can be the leading indicator of a market. Snapchat's first investor found it through a teenager.
- How founders divide credit and ownership at the start shapes everything that follows.
- Refusing a large acquisition offer requires conviction that the company can be worth more on its own.
- A company that promises privacy will be held to the promise.
- Features can be copied. A company that dominates by product alone must keep inventing.

## An outsider's account

Billy Gallagher wrote about Snapchat for TechCrunch from about 2012 to 2014, beginning as a Stanford student, and one of his early pieces profiled the company. *How to Turn Down a Billion Dollars*, published by St. Martin's Press in 2018, tells the company's story.

Gallagher was given no official access. Snap did not take part, so he could not interview Spiegel or his senior colleagues for the book, and the company's communications staff helped only with checking facts. He relied on sources from his years covering the company, on cold calls and emails, and on documents including leaked emails and court filings.

The book follows Snapchat from its founding in 2011 to its stock market listing in 2017.

Implication: read it as a reporter's reconstruction from the outside, stronger on the early years he covered than on a company that later closed its doors to him.

## Picaboo

The idea began at Stanford University. Reggie Brown, a student, proposed an app for sending photos that would disappear after they were viewed to his fraternity brother at Kappa Sigma, Evan Spiegel. Bobby Murphy, a friend who could code, built it. They launched it in 2011 as Picaboo, and later renamed it Snapchat.

Ephemerality was the founding idea. On Facebook, every photo became a permanent public record; on Snapchat, a message vanished. That made it feel more like conversation than publishing, and it suited people who wanted to share casual, unflattering or private moments without leaving a trace.

The mechanism is that a product that removes a hidden cost of existing tools, here the permanence of everything posted, can unlock behavior that people had been suppressing.

Implication: look for the costs that users of existing products accept without noticing, and ask what they would do if those costs disappeared.

## The cofounder cut out

The partnership did not last. In August 2011, after a falling-out, Spiegel and Murphy cut Brown out of the company, changing the passwords to the app's accounts. Brown sued in 2013, claiming he was owed a share of the business he had helped create.

The case was settled in 2014 for $157.5 million in cash, with no equity, an amount made public in the company's 2017 filing to go public. In the settlement statement, the company acknowledged Brown's contribution to creating Snapchat.

The mechanism is that disputes over who did what at a company's founding grow more expensive as the company grows, because the stakes rise while memories and documents remain unclear.

Implication: if you start a company with others, put ownership and roles in writing at the beginning, when the stakes are small.

## Teenagers first

Snapchat's early growth came from high-school students, who used it to send each other silly, disappearing photos. Its first investor found it through them. Barry Eggers, a partner at the venture firm Lightspeed, learned from his teenage daughter that her classmates used three apps, and Snapchat was one. His colleague Jeremy Liew tracked the company down when it had fewer than 100,000 users, and in 2012 Lightspeed was the only investor in a seed round of $485,000.

Facebook noticed too. In December 2012 it launched Poke, a close copy of Snapchat. Poke flopped.

The mechanism is that teenagers adopt new forms of communication first, because they have the most time, the strongest need for privacy from adults and the least attachment to existing tools.

Implication: watch what the youngest users of a technology adopt, because it often shows where the rest of the market will go.

## Saying no

In November 2013, the *Wall Street Journal* reported that Facebook had offered to buy Snapchat for about $3 billion in cash, and that Spiegel, then 23, had turned it down. The decision, which gives the book its title, seemed reckless to many observers. The company had little revenue.

Gallagher presents the refusal as an expression of Spiegel's conviction that Snapchat could become a major independent company, and that selling would subordinate his product vision to Facebook's.

The mechanism is that a founder who believes a company's long-term value far exceeds a current offer, and who controls the decision, can refuse money that would make most people rich.

Implication: before selling, estimate what the business could be worth if it succeeds on its own, and weigh how much you are willing to bet on that estimate.

## Growing up in public

As Snapchat grew, its founder's past and its promises came under scrutiny. In May 2014, the website Valleywag published crude, misogynistic emails Spiegel had written as a member of his fraternity. He apologized, saying he was mortified and that they did not reflect who he was or his views of women.

The same month, Snapchat settled charges from the Federal Trade Commission that it had misled users about how its messages disappeared. Recipients could save the images in several ways, and the company had collected some user data without disclosing it. Snapchat admitted no wrongdoing, paid no fine and agreed to twenty years of independent privacy monitoring.

The mechanism is that a product built on a promise, here that messages vanish, invites people to test whether the promise is true.

Implication: if your product's appeal rests on a promise, make sure it is technically true, because users and regulators will check.

## Stories and the copycat

Snapchat kept inventing. In 2013 it introduced Stories, collections of photos and videos that lasted twenty-four hours, followed in 2015 by Discover, a section for media publishers. In 2016 the company renamed itself Snap Inc. and announced Spectacles, camera glasses.

In March 2017, Snap went public at a valuation of about $24 billion, selling shares that carried no votes, a first for a major American listing, which kept control with its founders.

But in August 2016, Facebook's Instagram had launched its own Stories, a near-copy. Snapchat's growth in daily users slowed sharply in the following months, and within a year Instagram Stories had more daily users than Snapchat itself. Snap attributed its slowdown to other causes, including technical problems.

The mechanism is that a feature without network effects or other protections can be copied by a rival that already has a larger audience.

Implication: if your advantage is a product feature, assume larger competitors will copy it, and keep building what they cannot.

## Limits

- It lacks inside access. Without interviews with Spiegel or his senior team, the later chapters rely on earlier reporting, leaked emails and secondary sources, and reviewers found the sourcing thinner as the company grew more private.
- It leans toward admiration. *Publishers Weekly* noted that readers may not share Gallagher's view of Spiegel as brilliant, and a *Washington Post* review found moments of hero worship.
- It ends in 2017. It stops before Snapchat's troubled 2018 redesign and its later recovery, so its account of the contest with Instagram stops partway through.
