## Thesis

Far from making knowledge freer, the modern economy and the security state are steadily removing the most valuable knowledge from public reach, turning it into private property or state secrets, so that learning and reasoning about certain subjects, even independently, is becoming dangerous or illegal.

## The main ideas

- The most valuable information is increasingly private property or a state secret.
- Broad areas of physics and biology are kept out of public discussion as national security risks.
- Patents now cover not only inventions but sales techniques, hiring strategies and gene sequences.
- Copyright law makes it illegal to break the locks on digital content or to share the tools for doing so.
- Information is often labeled technical so that it can be locked away, rather than locked away because it is technical.
- An innocent insight, reached independently, can infringe a patent or threaten national security.
- The result, Laughlin warns, could be a new dark age in which useful knowledge belongs to a few.

## A physicist's warning

Robert B. Laughlin is a physicist at Stanford University who shared the 1998 Nobel Prize in Physics with Horst Störmer and Daniel Tsui for explaining the fractional quantum Hall effect, a strange collective behavior of electrons. His earlier book, *A Different Universe*, argued that many fundamental laws of physics emerge from the collective behavior of matter.

*The Crime of Reason*, published by Basic Books in 2008, is a different kind of book: a short, polemical essay of under 200 pages for general readers. Its subject is not physics but the law and economics of knowledge, and its title refers to the idea that some intellectual activities are becoming antisocial or illegal.

Implication: read it as a scientist's warning about the conditions for discovery, not as a legal treatise.

## Secrets of state

Laughlin's first example is national security. Broad areas of physics and biology, he argues, have been placed off limits to public discussion because they might help an enemy build weapons.

The clearest precedent is nuclear secrecy. Under American law since the Atomic Energy Act of 1946, information about nuclear weapons has been treated as restricted from the moment it exists, a doctrine known as "born secret," regardless of who produced it or how. In 1979 the government went to court to stop *The Progressive* magazine from publishing an article on the hydrogen bomb that its author had compiled from public sources.

For Laughlin, the danger is that this logic spreads. Once some knowledge is treated as dangerous in itself, other fields, such as biology, can be subjected to the same restrictions.

The mechanism is that a security regime designed to control a few weapons secrets tends to expand, because more knowledge can always be described as potentially dangerous.

Implication: when a field of knowledge is placed beyond public discussion for security reasons, ask how far the restriction extends and who decides its limits.

## Knowledge as property

Laughlin's second concern is economic. The most valuable information in a modern economy, he argues, is increasingly private property. Patents, once granted for physical inventions, now cover business methods such as sales techniques and hiring strategies, and even gene sequences. Companies can legally monopolize methods of communication.

As a result, a person who independently works out a useful idea may find that someone else already owns it, and that using it infringes their rights.

The mechanism is that when information becomes a valuable asset, its owners have strong incentives to extend and enforce their rights over it, steadily shrinking the public domain.

Implication: before building on an idea, check who may already own it, and consider how much of what you know you are free to use.

## Breaking the locks

Copyright adds another layer. Laws such as the American Digital Millennium Copyright Act of 1998 make it illegal to circumvent the technical measures that protect digital content, and to distribute software that breaks them.

Laughlin argues that this turns certain kinds of technical understanding, such as knowing how a protection system works and how to defeat it, into potential crimes, regardless of the purpose. Cryptography, biotechnology and software design are among the fields where he sees knowledge being restricted.

The mechanism is that laws written to protect content end up restricting knowledge about the tools that protect it.

Implication: notice when the law treats understanding how a system works as equivalent to misusing it.

## A new dark age

Laughlin's broadest claim is that society is sequestering knowledge more rapidly and thoroughly than ever before. He argues that information is often called technical as a way to justify locking it away, rather than being locked away because it is technical.

The fear, expressed in a jacket endorsement by the investor Peter Thiel, is of a new dark age in which scientific and technical knowledge becomes the province of experts, and the public is left with abundant information that is not what it needs.

The mechanism is that the knowledge with the greatest value, whether economic or military, is precisely the knowledge its holders most want to restrict, so the most useful information is the least available.

Implication: distinguish between the amount of information available and access to the information that matters. Abundance of the first can disguise scarcity of the second.

## Limits

- It argues by anecdote. Reviewers noted that the book leans on stories rather than systematic evidence, and that its case on intellectual property law is thinly researched and outside its author's expertise.
- It presents the choice too starkly. Critics such as Timothy B. Lee argued that the alternative to strict patent and copyright rules is not no protection, and that a legal system can protect security and innovation while keeping research open.
- Some of its examples have dated. American controls on exporting cryptography were largely relaxed by 2000, and in 2013 the Supreme Court ruled in *Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics* that naturally occurring DNA sequences cannot be patented.
