## Thesis

What people buy, eat, wear, give and enjoy is shaped by an evolved human nature, so consumer behavior that looks arbitrary or purely cultural, from craving fatty food to buying sports cars to giving gifts, follows the logic of four Darwinian drives: survival, reproduction, care for kin and cooperation with friends.

## The main ideas

- Consumption maps onto four evolved drives: survival, reproduction, kin selection and reciprocal altruism.
- Tastes that served survival in scarcity, such as the craving for calorie-dense food, persist in abundance.
- Much consumption is mating display. Luxury goods signal status and resources to potential partners and rivals.
- Hormones and the menstrual cycle can shift spending and behavior in measurable ways.
- Gifts follow the logic of relatedness and reciprocity.
- Cultural products, from song lyrics to romance novels, are fossils of the human mind.
- Marketing and social science that treat the mind as a blank slate miss the biology that drives choices.

## Homo consumericus

Gad Saad, a professor of marketing at Concordia University in Montreal since 1994, is a founder of evolutionary consumer psychology, the study of consumption through the lens of evolutionary biology. He set out the field's framework in an academic book, *The Evolutionary Bases of Consumption* (2007), and wrote a blog for *Psychology Today* titled "Homo Consumericus." *The Consuming Instinct*, published by Prometheus Books in 2011, presents the same argument for general readers.

His framework is organized around four Darwinian modules. Survival covers food and safety. Reproduction covers mating and display. Kin selection covers spending on family, in proportion to genetic relatedness. Reciprocal altruism covers exchanges with friends and allies. Each, he argues, accounts for consumer behavior that economists and marketers usually explain in other ways.

Saad later became a prominent public commentator, and his book *The Parasitic Mind* (2020) turned to political and cultural criticism.

Implication: when you try to explain a purchase, ask which of the four drives it serves.

## Survival and appetite

The subtitle's juicy burgers stand for the first module. Human tastes evolved in environments where calories were scarce and unpredictable, so a strong preference for fatty, sweet, energy-dense food helped people survive. In modern environments where such food is cheap and everywhere, the same preference drives overeating.

In Saad's framework, food marketing succeeds by appealing to these evolved preferences, and problems such as compulsive eating and buying reflect a mismatch between ancient instincts and a modern market.

The mechanism is that preferences shaped by natural selection respond to the cues that once predicted survival, even when those cues now lead to harm.

Implication: treat strong cravings for certain foods as ancient signals operating in the wrong environment, not as reliable guides to what your body needs.

## Ferraris and display

The reproduction module explains much conspicuous consumption. Drawing on the economist Thorstein Veblen's idea of conspicuous consumption and on biologists' theories of costly signaling, of which the peacock's tail is the classic example, Saad argues that luxury goods often serve as displays of status and resources to potential mates and rivals.

His own research tested one version of the idea. In a 2009 study with John Vongas, 39 young men each drove an expensive Porsche sports car and an old Toyota Camry station wagon. Their testosterone levels rose after driving the Porsche and did not change significantly after driving the Camry.

The mechanism is that goods which are expensive and visible work as signals precisely because they are costly, so only those with resources can display them.

Implication: when a purchase seems wildly beyond its practical value, ask whom it is meant to impress.

## Hormones and spending

Evolutionary consumer research, which Saad champions, also looks for effects of the menstrual cycle. In a widely reported 2007 study, Geoffrey Miller, Joshua Tybur and Brent Jordan found that professional lap dancers in Albuquerque earned much higher tips near ovulation than at other points in their cycles, while dancers taking hormonal contraceptives showed no such peak. Other studies from the same period found that women near ovulation chose more appearance-enhancing clothing and products.

The argument is that women's behavior shifts subtly with fertility, and men respond to the shift, even when neither is aware of it.

The mechanism, in this account, is that hormones tied to reproduction affect appearance, behavior and choices in ways that consumers and marketers rarely notice.

Implication: be open to the possibility that some of your choices are influenced by biological states you do not perceive, while recognizing that much of this research is still contested.

## Gifts and kin

The kin and reciprocity modules explain giving. In a 2003 study, Saad and Tripat Gill found that young adults spent the largest share of their gift budgets on romantic partners, that men reported more strategic motives for giving, and that spending on relatives rose with genetic closeness.

In Saad's framework, gifts to relatives follow kin selection, investment in those who share one's genes; gifts to partners follow the logic of courtship; and gifts to friends follow reciprocity, the expectation of exchange over time.

The mechanism is that giving, which seems a matter of sentiment, follows predictable patterns that reflect relatedness, mating interest and the expectation of return.

Implication: look at your own gift-giving and ask what pattern it follows, and what it signals to the people who receive it.

## Fossils of the mind

Saad argues that cultural products, such as song lyrics, film plots, romance novels and pornography, are fossils of the human mind. Because they succeed by appealing to large audiences, they reveal what people across cultures want.

The subtitle's pornography points to one of his themes: products consumed mainly by men and those consumed mainly by women, such as romance novels, differ in ways that he reads as reflecting different evolved priorities in mating.

The mechanism is that markets select for content that appeals to evolved preferences, so what sells widely is evidence about human nature.

Implication: when a type of story or product sells across many cultures, look for the universal human preference it satisfies.

## Against the blank slate

The book's polemical target is what Saad calls the blank-slate view in consumer research and social science: the assumption that people's desires are created by culture, socialization and advertising. In this view, marketers shape consumers.

Saad argues that consumers arrive with evolved preferences that advertising can exploit but not create. Marketers who ignore biology misunderstand why their campaigns work, and social scientists who ignore it miss the deepest causes of behavior. He calls for combining biological and cultural explanations rather than choosing between them.

The mechanism is that a theory which treats preferences as purely learned cannot explain why the same preferences appear across cultures and even in other species.

Implication: when explaining a consumer trend, look for the evolved preference beneath the cultural surface before assuming it was created by advertising.

## Limits

- Much of its evidence rests on small studies. Its headline findings include experiments with 39 men and data from 18 dancers, and many evolutionary explanations of consumption are plausible after the fact without being tested against alternatives.
- The research on cycle effects has weakened. Larger, preregistered studies published since 2018 have found little or no shift in women's preferences across the cycle, though some changes in desire have held up.
- Its opponents may be a straw man. Critics argue that few social scientists ever held a pure blank-slate view, so the contrast between biology and culture is sharper in the book than in the field.
