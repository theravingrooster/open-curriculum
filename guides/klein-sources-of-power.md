## Thesis

Experienced people under time pressure do not decide by comparing options; they recognize the situation, which brings a workable action to mind, and they check that single action by imagining it being carried out.

## The main ideas

- Experts rarely weigh alternatives. In a study of fire commanders, over 80% of decisions involved no comparison of options at all.
- Recognizing a situation as typical supplies goals, cues to watch, expectations and a typical action, all at once.
- Intuition is pattern recognition built from experience, and it often registers a problem before the person can say what is wrong.
- Experts see things novices cannot: cues, anomalies and the absence of expected events.
- An expert evaluates an option by mentally simulating it, and adjusts or rejects it if the simulation fails.
- Formal analysis of options has its place, but it is slower, and it misleads when forced onto problems where experience works better.

## Recognition-primed decisions

Klein's research began in 1985 with a study of fireground commanders, funded by the Army Research Institute. His team expected to find commanders comparing two or three options under pressure. They interviewed 26 experienced commanders, with an average of 23 years in the job, about 156 decision points in real incidents. In over 80% of those decisions, the commanders had not compared options. They had seen what to do.

From this Klein built the recognition-primed decision model. An experienced person recognizes a situation as an instance of a familiar type. That recognition brings four things with it: plausible goals, the cues that matter, expectations of what should happen next, and a typical course of action. The person does not generate alternatives; they take the first workable option and evaluate it on its own, which Klein calls singular evaluation.

The model has three variations. In the simplest, the situation is recognized and the typical action is taken. In the second, the situation is unclear, so the effort goes into diagnosing it, and once it is diagnosed the action follows. In the third, the situation is clear but the action is doubtful, so the person imagines carrying it out to see whether it will work.

Implication: in fast-moving situations, the useful question is not "what are my options?" but "what kind of situation is this, and what would I expect to see if I am right?"

## The power of intuition

Klein defines intuition as the use of experience to recognize patterns, not as a mysterious sense. His signature case is a lieutenant leading a crew into a one-storey house with a fire in the back, in the kitchen area. The crew sprayed water on it, and it had little effect. The room was hotter than it should have been and quieter than it should have been. Uneasy without knowing why, the lieutenant ordered everyone out. Moments later, the floor where they had been standing collapsed. The fire had been burning in the basement, which he had not known existed.

The lieutenant credited a sixth sense. Klein's reconstruction showed something more specific: the situation violated his expectations. A kitchen fire should respond to water, should not be that hot, and should be noisy. The mismatch between what he expected and what he perceived was the signal, even though he could not name it at the time.

The mechanism is that experience builds a store of patterns, including what a situation normally looks, sounds and feels like. The sense that something is wrong is the pattern failing to match.

Implication: when an experienced person says a situation feels wrong, treat it as data. Ask which expectation is being violated, and what the explanation might be.

## The power to see the invisible

Experts perceive things that novices cannot see even when they are shown them: fine distinctions, patterns across cues, the history of how a situation got here, and above all the absence of something that should be present. Klein counts the ability to notice what did not happen as a mark of expertise.

The evidence includes work by Klein's colleagues Beth Crandall and K. Getchell-Reiter with nurses in neonatal intensive care. Experienced nurses could often tell that an infant was developing sepsis, a life-threatening infection, before test results confirmed it. At first the nurses could not say how they knew. Structured interviews, using what Klein's team calls the critical decision method, brought out the specific cues they had been reading without putting them into words.

Implication: expert knowledge is often tacit, and it can be drawn out by asking experts to walk through specific incidents in detail rather than asking them for general rules. Training that shows novices what experts notice passes on more than training in procedures.

## The power of mental simulation

To evaluate a single option, experts imagine it being carried out, step by step, and watch for where it breaks. If the simulation runs smoothly, they act. If it hits a problem, they modify the plan or discard it and simulate the next one. Mental simulation is also how experts diagnose, by building a story of how a situation could have arisen and checking it against the facts.

Klein's case is a rescue. A woman had fallen or jumped from a highway overpass and landed, semiconscious, on the metal struts of a highway sign. The lieutenant in charge sent a crew below to block traffic, then considered how to lift her off. He imagined using a Kingsley harness, which snaps onto the shoulders and thighs, and rejected it: fitting it would mean lifting her to a sitting position on a narrow strut, where she could fall. He imagined a ladder belt instead, which needed her lifted only an inch or two to buckle it, and chose that. He had evaluated both options, but one at a time, by running each through in his head.

Implication: before committing to a plan, run it through in imagination from start to finish, in concrete detail, and look for the step where it fails. The plan that survives the simulation is usually good enough to act on.

## The first option is usually good

The recognition-primed model depends on a claim that sounds risky: that the first option an expert thinks of is usually a good one. Klein tested it with chess players. In a study with Wolf, Militello and Zsambok, the first moves that players thought of were mostly strong, rated 3.59 on average on a five-point scale. In an earlier study with Calderwood and Crandall, master players kept the quality of their moves in blitz games at about six seconds a move, while weaker, class-B players did not.

The mechanism is that experience narrows attention to the options that fit the situation; poor options never come to mind. This is why speed does not destroy an expert's performance but does destroy a novice's: the expert's first option comes from recognition, the novice's from guessing.

Implication: under time pressure, an experienced person should act on the first option that survives a quick mental simulation rather than generating a list. Comparing options is a strategy for people who lack the experience to recognize the situation.

## The power of rational analysis

Klein does not reject formal decision analysis, in which options are listed, criteria weighted and scores compared. He argues that it is the right tool in specific conditions: when there is enough time; when the decision must be justified to others; when parties with different priorities need to resolve a conflict; when the goal is the best possible option rather than one that works; when the problem is too computationally complex for intuition; and when the decision maker lacks experience.

The danger he names is hyperrationality: applying analytic procedures where they do not fit, discarding the knowledge of experienced people because it cannot be written as a formula. Analysis breaks a situation into parts and loses the patterns that experts use; it is also slow, and in a fire, a battle or an emergency room, the situation changes before the analysis is done.

Implication: use formal comparison for decisions that are slow, contested, novel or combinatorially complex, and trust trained intuition, checked by simulation, in fast, familiar ones.

## Limits

- Klein's evidence comes largely from the critical decision method, in which experts recall and describe past incidents. Retrospective accounts are subject to memory errors and to the tendency to make past decisions sound more coherent than they were.
- The model describes experts in environments that give them repeated practice with clear feedback. Kahneman and Klein, in "Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree" (American Psychologist, 2009), agreed that intuition can be trusted only in such high-validity environments, and not in domains such as long-range forecasting or stock picking, where feedback is delayed or noisy.
- Recognition fails on genuinely novel situations, which are also the ones where mistakes are costliest. The book shows experts succeeding on familiar patterns; it offers less on how to tell, in the moment, that a situation only looks familiar.
