## Thesis

At the smallest scales, nature does not behave like anything in everyday experience, and the only way to understand it is to learn its rules directly: events are governed by probability amplitudes that combine and interfere, and from a few such rules, applied to simple systems, follow the behavior of atoms, molecules, crystals, semiconductors and superconductors.

## The main ideas

- The two-slit experiment contains the central mystery of quantum mechanics, and no classical picture can explain it.
- Quantum events are governed by probability amplitudes, which add and interfere before they are squared into probabilities.
- Identical particles behave in ways that distinguishable ones do not.
- Simple systems with only two possible states, such as a spinning electron or an ammonia molecule, teach the logic of quantum mechanics most clearly.
- The same principles explain the behavior of crystals, semiconductors and atoms.
- Quantum effects can appear on a large scale, as in superconductivity.
- Physics is part of the true culture of modern times.

## The heart of quantum mechanics

Volume III of *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, published in 1965, covers the quantum mechanics Richard Feynman taught at the end of his two-year course at Caltech, edited by Robert B. Leighton and Matthew Sands. It is free to read online, with the other volumes, at [The Feynman Lectures on Physics website](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/).

It opens with the two-slit experiment, which Feynman describes as a phenomenon "impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics." Electrons sent one at a time through two slits arrive at a screen like particles, in individual spots, but build up the interference pattern of a wave. If the electrons are observed to see which slit each passes through, the pattern disappears.

Feynman does not try to explain the mystery away. He says no one knows how nature does this, and that the task is to describe accurately what happens.

Implication: when the evidence contradicts every familiar picture, describe it accurately rather than forcing it into a picture that does not fit.

## Amplitudes

The rule behind the two-slit experiment is the core of the volume. Each way an event can happen has a probability amplitude, a complex number. When an event can happen in several indistinguishable ways, the amplitudes are added first and the result squared to give the probability. Because amplitudes can cancel, adding a second path can make an outcome less likely, which is the source of interference.

When the ways can be distinguished, as when someone checks which slit the electron used, the probabilities are added instead, and the interference vanishes.

Feynman extends this to identical particles. Because two electrons, or two photons, cannot be told apart, exchanging them must be counted as the same outcome, and this leads to the different behavior of the two families of particles, bosons and fermions.

The mechanism is that quantum probabilities come from combining amplitudes, not probabilities, so possibilities can cancel each other.

Implication: in quantum mechanics, what can in principle be known about a process changes its outcome.

## Two-state systems

The volume's approach is unusual. Most courses begin with the Schrödinger equation, which describes particles as waves in space. Feynman begins instead with systems that have only a few possible states, such as the spin of a particle, which can point up or down, and builds the theory from the rules for how amplitudes for those states change over time.

He introduces the Hamiltonian matrix, which governs that change, and works through example after example of two-state systems. The Schrödinger equation does not appear until well past the middle of the volume.

The mechanism is that the logic of quantum mechanics is clearest in the simplest systems, where it is not obscured by the mathematics of waves in space.

Implication: when learning a difficult subject, start with the simplest case that shows its essential logic, and add complications afterward.

## The ammonia maser

One of Feynman's main examples is the ammonia molecule. Its nitrogen atom can sit on either side of the plane of its three hydrogen atoms, giving two states. Because the molecule can pass between them, the true states of definite energy are combinations of the two, separated by a small energy difference.

Feynman shows how this two-state picture explains the ammonia maser, a device that amplifies microwaves by sending molecules in the higher energy state through a cavity, and the forerunner of the laser.

The mechanism is that a system that can switch between two configurations has stationary states that are combinations of both, with slightly different energies, and transitions between them absorb or emit radiation of a precise frequency.

Implication: a simple model, worked through carefully, can explain a working technology.

## Crystals and semiconductors

From two-state systems, Feynman moves to an electron that can hop between atoms in a crystal. The amplitudes for its possible positions combine into waves that move through the lattice, and the allowed energies form bands.

This leads to semiconductors, the materials of transistors. Feynman explains how impurities add or remove electrons, how the missing electrons, called holes, behave like positive charges, and how junctions between different semiconductors control the flow of current. Later chapters return to the hydrogen atom and the structure of the periodic table.

The mechanism is that the quantum rules for a single particle, applied to a regular array of atoms, explain why some materials conduct electricity and others do not.

Implication: the technology of modern electronics rests directly on quantum mechanics, not merely on its approximations.

## Superconductivity

The final chapter, presented as a seminar on superconductivity, applies the Schrödinger equation in a classical context. In a superconductor, cooled to very low temperatures, vast numbers of electrons pair up and occupy the same quantum state, so that the wave function, normally a description of a single particle, behaves like a large-scale field.

The result is that quantum mechanics becomes visible on the scale of everyday objects: currents that flow without resistance and magnetic effects governed by quantum rules.

The mechanism is that when many particles share one quantum state, their collective behavior shows quantum effects that are normally hidden by averaging.

Implication: quantum mechanics is not confined to the microscopic world. Under the right conditions, its strangeness becomes directly observable.

## The epilogue

The course ends with Feynman's farewell. "Well, I've been talking to you for two years and now I'm going to quit." He tells the students that his aim had been less to prepare them for examinations than to give them some appreciation of the wonderful world and the physicist's way of looking at it, which, he believed, "is a major part of the true culture of modern times."

It is a modest ending to an ambitious course. Feynman knew that much of what he had taught would be hard for most of his students to absorb, but he hoped that the way of looking at the world would last longer than the details.

Implication: study science not only for its uses but as part of the culture of the age, a way of seeing the world as it is.

## Limits

- Its approach is unusual and difficult. Beginning with amplitudes and two-state systems rather than the Schrödinger equation makes it an unconventional first course, and many readers find it most valuable after a standard textbook.
- It predates later developments. It was taught before experimental tests of Bell's inequality and the growth of quantum information science, which have changed how quantum foundations are discussed and applied.
- It is not a problem book. It explains the logic of quantum mechanics brilliantly but gives little practice with the calculations a working physicist needs.
