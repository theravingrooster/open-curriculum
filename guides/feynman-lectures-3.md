## Thesis

At the smallest scales, nature does not behave like anything in everyday experience, and the only way to understand it is to learn its rules directly: events are governed by probability amplitudes that combine and interfere, and from a few such rules, applied to simple systems, follow the behavior of atoms, molecules, crystals, semiconductors and superconductors.

## The main ideas

- The two-slit experiment contains the central mystery of quantum mechanics, and no classical picture can explain it.
- Quantum events are governed by probability amplitudes, which add and interfere before they are squared into probabilities.
- Identical particles behave in ways that distinguishable ones do not; bosons and fermions follow different rules.
- Simple systems with only two possible states, such as a spinning electron or an ammonia molecule, teach the logic of quantum mechanics most clearly.
- The Hamiltonian matrix governs how amplitudes change with time.
- The same principles explain the behavior of crystals, semiconductors and the hydrogen atom.
- Quantum effects can appear on a large scale, as in superconductivity.
- Symmetry and conservation laws constrain the form of the quantum rules.
- Physics is part of the true culture of modern times.

## The heart of quantum mechanics

Volume III of *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, published in 1965, covers the quantum mechanics Richard Feynman taught at the end of his two-year course at Caltech, edited by Robert B. Leighton and Matthew Sands. It is free to read online, with the other volumes, at [The Feynman Lectures on Physics website](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/).

It opens with the two-slit experiment, which Feynman describes as a phenomenon "impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics." Electrons sent one at a time through two slits arrive at a screen like particles, in individual spots, but build up the interference pattern of a wave. If the electrons are observed to see which slit each passes through, the pattern disappears.

Feynman does not try to explain the mystery away. He says no one knows how nature does this, and that the task is to describe accurately what happens. He contrasts the experiment with bullets (which produce two peaks) and with water waves (which produce an interference pattern) and shows that electrons fit neither description.

Implication: when the evidence contradicts every familiar picture, describe it accurately rather than forcing it into a picture that does not fit. The two-slit experiment is the clearest such case in physics.

## Amplitudes

The rule behind the two-slit experiment is the core of the volume. Each way an event can happen has a probability amplitude, a complex number. When an event can happen in several indistinguishable ways, the amplitudes are added first and the result squared to give the probability. Because amplitudes can cancel, adding a second path can make an outcome less likely, which is the source of interference.

When the ways can be distinguished, as when someone checks which slit the electron used, the probabilities are added instead, and the interference vanishes. Feynman states the rules precisely: the probability is the absolute square of the amplitude; amplitudes for alternative indistinguishable paths are added; amplitudes for successive events are multiplied.

The mechanism is that quantum probabilities come from combining amplitudes, not probabilities, so possibilities can cancel each other. The complex nature of the amplitudes allows constructive and destructive interference.

Implication: in quantum mechanics, what can in principle be known about a process changes its outcome. Distinguishability is not a minor detail; it determines whether interference occurs.

## Identical particles

Feynman extends the amplitude rules to identical particles. Because two electrons, or two photons, cannot be told apart, exchanging them must be counted as the same outcome. For bosons (integer spin) the amplitudes for the two exchange paths are added; for fermions (half-integer spin) they are subtracted. The subtraction leads to the exclusion principle: no two fermions can occupy the same state.

He works through the consequences for blackbody radiation (photons are bosons), for liquid helium (helium-4 atoms are bosons and can form a superfluid) and for the structure of atoms (electrons are fermions). The different statistics are not additional postulates; they follow from the requirement that identical particles be indistinguishable.

The mechanism is that the exchange of two identical particles is not a new physical process; it is the same process counted twice or with a relative minus sign. The sign difference produces the entire distinction between Bose and Fermi statistics.

Implication: the behavior of large numbers of identical particles—lasers, superconductors, the periodic table—follows from the single rule that exchanging two identical particles multiplies the amplitude by +1 or –1.

## Two-state systems and the Hamiltonian

The volume's approach is unusual. Most courses begin with the Schrödinger equation, which describes particles as waves in space. Feynman begins instead with systems that have only a few possible states, such as the spin of a particle, which can point up or down, and builds the theory from the rules for how amplitudes for those states change over time.

He introduces the Hamiltonian matrix, which governs that change. If the amplitudes for the base states are written as a column vector, the time derivative of the vector is (–i/ℏ) times the Hamiltonian matrix times the vector. He works through example after example of two-state systems: a spin in a magnetic field, the ammonia molecule, and coupled spins. The Schrödinger equation does not appear until well past the middle of the volume.

The mechanism is that the logic of quantum mechanics is clearest in the simplest systems, where it is not obscured by the mathematics of waves in space. Once the rules for amplitudes and the Hamiltonian are understood for two states, the extension to more states is mainly technical.

Implication: when learning a difficult subject, start with the simplest case that shows its essential logic, and add complications afterward. Two-state systems are that simplest case for quantum mechanics.

## The ammonia maser

One of Feynman's main examples is the ammonia molecule. Its nitrogen atom can sit on either side of the plane of its three hydrogen atoms, giving two states. Because the molecule can pass between them, the true states of definite energy are combinations of the two, separated by a small energy difference. The energy splitting is on the order of 10⁻⁴ electron volts, corresponding to microwave frequencies.

Feynman shows how this two-state picture explains the ammonia maser, a device that amplifies microwaves by sending molecules in the higher energy state through a cavity, and the forerunner of the laser. An electric field shifts the energies and allows the selection of molecules in the upper state; those molecules then emit into the cavity mode.

The mechanism is that a system that can switch between two configurations has stationary states that are combinations of both, with slightly different energies, and transitions between them absorb or emit radiation of a precise frequency. The maser is a practical use of that frequency.

Implication: a simple model, worked through carefully, can explain a working technology. The ammonia maser is the concrete demonstration that the two-state analysis is not merely formal.

## Spin and angular momentum

Feynman treats spin as a two-state system (or, for spin 1, a three-state system) and derives the rules for how spin amplitudes transform under rotation. He introduces the Pauli matrices for spin ½ and shows that the expectation value of the spin precesses in a magnetic field at the Larmor frequency. The same mathematics describes the polarization of light and the behavior of neutral K mesons.

He connects spin to angular momentum and notes that the quantum rules for angular momentum follow from the commutation relations of the rotation operators. The Stern–Gerlach experiment is used as the concrete illustration of spin measurement and of the non-classical behavior of successive measurements.

The mechanism is that the transformation properties of amplitudes under rotation determine the possible spin values and the selection rules for transitions. The mathematics is the same whether the system is an electron spin, a photon polarization or a molecular orientation.

Implication: spin is not an extra classical rotation; it is a quantum degree of freedom whose behavior is fixed by the rotation group. Once the transformation rules are known, the magnetic and optical consequences follow.

## Crystals, semiconductors and the hydrogen atom

From two-state systems, Feynman moves to an electron that can hop between atoms in a crystal. The amplitudes for its possible positions combine into waves that move through the lattice, and the allowed energies form bands. Gaps appear when the lattice is periodic; the gaps determine whether the material is a metal, an insulator or a semiconductor.

This leads to semiconductors, the materials of transistors. Feynman explains how impurities add or remove electrons, how the missing electrons, called holes, behave like positive charges, and how junctions between different semiconductors control the flow of current. Later chapters return to the hydrogen atom and the structure of the periodic table, showing how the quantum numbers n, l and m arise from the solution of the Schrödinger equation in a Coulomb potential.

The mechanism is that the quantum rules for a single particle, applied to a regular array of atoms, explain why some materials conduct electricity and others do not. The band structure is the collective consequence of the hopping amplitudes.

Implication: the technology of modern electronics rests directly on quantum mechanics, not merely on its approximations. Transistors and the periodic table are both applications of the same amplitude rules.

## Superconductivity

The final chapter, presented as a seminar on superconductivity, applies the Schrödinger equation in a classical context. In a superconductor, cooled to very low temperatures, vast numbers of electrons pair up and occupy the same quantum state, so that the wave function, normally a description of a single particle, behaves like a large-scale field. The pairing is mediated by the lattice vibrations; the resulting condensate has a macroscopic phase.

The result is that quantum mechanics becomes visible on the scale of everyday objects: currents that flow without resistance and magnetic effects governed by quantum rules (the Meissner effect and flux quantization). Feynman sketches the London equations and the idea of a macroscopic wave function.

The mechanism is that when many particles share one quantum state, their collective behavior shows quantum effects that are normally hidden by averaging. The superconducting condensate is a single quantum state occupied by 10²³ particles.

Implication: quantum mechanics is not confined to the microscopic world. Under the right conditions, its strangeness becomes directly observable on laboratory scales.

## Symmetry and the epilogue

Feynman treats symmetry as a constraint on the form of the quantum laws. Invariance under translations, rotations and time shifts implies conservation of momentum, angular momentum and energy. He notes the discovery that the weak interactions violate parity (mirror symmetry) and discusses the implications for the structure of the laws.

The course ends with Feynman's farewell. "Well, I've been talking to you for two years and now I'm going to quit." He tells the students that his aim had been less to prepare them for examinations than to give them some appreciation of the wonderful world and the physicist's way of looking at it, which, he believed, "is a major part of the true culture of modern times."

The mechanism is that symmetry requirements limit the possible terms in the Hamiltonian; once the symmetries are known, many otherwise arbitrary couplings are forbidden. The cultural claim is that the resulting picture of the world is part of what an educated person should know.

Implication: study science not only for its uses but as part of the culture of the age, a way of seeing the world as it is. The symmetry principles are among the most durable parts of that picture.

## Limits

- Its approach is unusual and difficult. Beginning with amplitudes and two-state systems rather than the Schrödinger equation makes it an unconventional first course, and many readers find it most valuable after a standard textbook.
- It predates later developments. It was taught before experimental tests of Bell's inequality and the growth of quantum information science, which have changed how quantum foundations are discussed and applied.
- It is not a problem book. It explains the logic of quantum mechanics brilliantly but gives little practice with the calculations a working physicist needs.
- The treatment of superconductivity is schematic; the microscopic BCS theory is not derived.
