# CrazyBusy
Edward M. Hallowell (2006)

Modern life produces a culturally induced attention-deficit state. The condition is manageable by deliberate choice of what to protect and what to cancel.

Key concepts: F-state · C-state · attention deficit trait · gemmelsmerch

## Overview

Hallowell, a psychiatrist who has written extensively on clinical ADHD, argues that a large fraction of working adults now exhibit a reversible version of the same pattern. He calls the reversible form **F-state**: frantic, frenzied, forgetful, frustrated, furious. In F-state, working memory collapses, people make more errors, and ordinary social friction escalates. The opposite is **C-state**: calm, clear, concentrated, curious, courteous. Multitasking and constant interruption drive people into F-state; the frontal systems that would otherwise prioritize and inhibit are overwhelmed. The same environment that produces the state also makes it feel normal, so people treat the symptoms as a personal failure rather than a predictable response to load.

The practical distinction is between clinical ADHD, which is lifelong and present from childhood, and the **attention deficit trait** that appears in adults under sustained overload. Hallowell estimates the trait affects a majority of knowledge workers. The mechanism is straightforward: each additional open loop (email, message, unfinished task) consumes working memory. When the number of open loops exceeds capacity, the brain defaults to reactive scanning. Labor-saving devices accelerate the process. By reducing the time required for any single action they increase the number of actions that can be started, so total load rises. Hallowell labels the ubiquitous low-grade distractors **gemmelsmerch**—the small interruptions that never feel decisive but steadily erode the C-state.

The implication is that recovery requires subtraction, not better time management inside the existing load. Hallowell's operational rules are to decide what matters most and schedule it first, to cancel activities that do not serve that decision, and to treat technology as a tool that can be turned off. The ten principles he lists (do what matters most, create a positive emotional environment, find rhythm, invest time for return, avoid screensucking, delegate, slow down, avoid ineffective multitasking, play) are applications of the same subtraction. The book is useful as a diagnostic frame more than as a novel technique; the C-state / F-state distinction lets a person notice the transition and interrupt it before the day is lost.

## Limits

The evidence is clinical observation and case material rather than controlled trials of the specific protocols. The claim that the modern workplace is "ADD-ogenic" is plausible but not quantified. Some of the language ("culturally induced ADD") blurs the line between a trait that responds to environment and a neurodevelopmental condition that does not. Readers with clinical ADHD will still need the separate diagnostic and treatment literature.
