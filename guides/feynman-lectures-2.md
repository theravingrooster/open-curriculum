## Thesis

Electricity, magnetism and light are one phenomenon, described completely by Maxwell's equations, and the clearest way to understand them is through fields that fill space; the same mathematics then explains the behavior of matter, from dielectrics and magnets to elastic solids and flowing water, because nature uses the same patterns again and again.

## The main ideas

- Maxwell's discovery of the laws of electrodynamics was the most significant event of the nineteenth century.
- Electric and magnetic forces are best understood through fields, which carry energy and momentum of their own.
- The same equations have the same solutions, so a problem solved in one field of physics is solved in every field governed by those equations.
- Gauss's law relates the flux of the electric field through a closed surface to the charge inside it.
- The vector potential, once treated as a mathematical convenience, is physically real in quantum mechanics.
- Nature's laws can be stated as a principle of least action.
- Induction and the displacement current complete Maxwell's equations and predict electromagnetic waves.
- The physics of matter, from magnets to fluids, follows from electromagnetism and the behavior of atoms.
- Curved space is the geometry of gravitation.

## Maxwell's equations

Volume II of *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, published in 1964 and subtitled *Mainly Electromagnetism and Matter*, covers the second year of the course Richard Feynman taught at Caltech from 1961 to 1963, edited by Robert B. Leighton and Matthew Sands. Like the other volumes, it is free to read online at [The Feynman Lectures on Physics website](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/).

Its first chapter makes a historical claim. From a long view of the history of mankind, seen from ten thousand years in the future, "the most significant event of the 19th century will be judged as Maxwell's discovery of the laws of electrodynamics." The American Civil War, Feynman adds, "will pale into provincial insignificance in comparison with this important scientific event of the same decade."

James Clerk Maxwell's equations, completed in the 1860s, united electricity, magnetism and light, predicted radio waves and became the foundation of electrical technology. Feynman states the four equations in integral and differential form and notes that they contain the entire content of classical electromagnetism.

Implication: measure the importance of an event by its consequences over centuries, not by how much attention it drew at the time. Maxwell's equations are the single most useful set of equations in classical physics.

## Fields

Feynman builds the volume around the idea of the field: a quantity defined at every point in space, such as the electric field, which tells what force a charge would feel there. Rather than thinking of charges acting on each other at a distance, physics describes charges creating fields and fields acting on charges. The electric field of a point charge falls as 1/r²; the magnetic field of a moving charge or a current is more complicated.

To work with fields, he first teaches the mathematics of vector calculus, the language of divergence, curl and flux in which Maxwell's equations are written. He defines the gradient, the divergence and the curl, and he shows how the flux of a vector field through a surface and the circulation around a loop are related to the divergence and curl inside the volume or surface by the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem.

The mechanism is that describing forces through fields turns a tangle of interactions between distant objects into local rules that hold at every point. The field at a point depends only on the sources and on the values of the field in the immediate neighborhood.

Implication: when relationships between distant parts of a system seem complicated, look for a description in terms of local quantities and local rules. Fields are the local description of electromagnetism.

## Gauss's law and electrostatics

Gauss's law states that the flux of the electric field through any closed surface is equal to the charge enclosed divided by ε₀. Feynman derives it from Coulomb's law and then uses it to find the field of symmetric charge distributions: a spherical shell, a uniform sphere, an infinite plane and a line charge. The law is powerful precisely because symmetry often makes the flux calculation trivial.

He also treats the electrostatic potential, the work required to assemble a charge distribution, and the energy stored in the electric field (½ε₀E² integrated over space). The field of a dipole, the method of images and the behavior of conductors in electrostatics are worked through as applications.

The mechanism is that for highly symmetric charge distributions the electric field is constant in magnitude on the Gaussian surface and perpendicular to it, so the flux is simply the field times the area. The enclosed charge then determines the field immediately.

Implication: when a charge distribution has symmetry, Gauss's law often gives the field without integration. The same technique reappears for gravity and for magnetic fields in appropriate cases.

## The same equations, the same solutions

In a chapter on electrostatic analogs, Feynman points out that the equations of electrostatics also describe steady heat flow, the stretching of a membrane, the diffusion of neutrons and other phenomena. Since the same equations have the same solutions, a problem solved in one of these fields is solved in all of them.

He then asks why nature should use the same equations so often, and suggests that the answer lies less in a deep unity of substance than in common approximations: smoothed-out descriptions of space that lead naturally to the same mathematical forms. Laplace's equation and the diffusion equation appear in many contexts for this reason.

The mechanism is that physical systems governed by the same mathematics behave alike, whatever they are made of. The mathematical structure, not the material substrate, determines the qualitative behavior.

Implication: when you face a new problem, ask whether its mathematical structure matches one that has already been solved, even in a very different field. Electrostatic solutions can be reused for heat flow or membrane problems.

## The vector potential

Electromagnetism can be described with a quantity called the vector potential as well as with electric and magnetic fields. For most of the history of physics, the potential was treated as a mathematical convenience, with only the fields counting as real. The magnetic field is the curl of the vector potential; the electric field involves both the gradient of the scalar potential and the time derivative of the vector potential.

Feynman argues otherwise. Using a then recent prediction now known as the Aharonov–Bohm effect, he shows that in quantum mechanics electrons can be affected by the vector potential in regions where the magnetic field is zero. In quantum mechanics, he argues, the vector potential is a real field, and force becomes a secondary idea. Some physicists later questioned details of his account, and others have defended it.

The mechanism is that a quantity that seems redundant in one theory can turn out to be fundamental in a deeper one. The classical fields can be derived from the potentials, but the potentials contain phase information that matters in quantum mechanics.

Implication: be cautious about dismissing parts of a description as mere bookkeeping. A deeper theory may show they matter. The vector potential is the clearest example in the volume.

## Induction and Maxwell's equations

Faraday's law states that a changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an electromotive force equal to the negative rate of change of the flux. Feynman derives the differential form (curl E = –∂B/∂t) and shows how it, together with the absence of magnetic monopoles, determines the electric field produced by a changing magnetic field.

Maxwell's addition of the displacement current (the term ε₀∂E/∂t in Ampère's law) makes the equations consistent with charge conservation and predicts electromagnetic waves that travel at the speed of light. Feynman writes the full set of Maxwell's equations and notes that they are the complete classical theory of electromagnetism.

The mechanism is that a changing magnetic field produces a curling electric field, and a changing electric field produces a curling magnetic field. The mutual induction of the two fields allows a self-sustaining wave to propagate.

Implication: the prediction of electromagnetic waves from the equations, before they were observed, is one of the strongest confirmations that the field description is correct. Radio, light and X-rays are all solutions of the same equations.

## The disk paradox

Feynman poses a puzzle that has come to bear his name. A disk free to rotate carries electric charges around its rim and a coil of current-carrying wire at its center. When the current is switched off, the changing magnetic field produces an electric field that pushes the charges and sets the disk turning. But angular momentum must be conserved, and nothing seems to have supplied it.

He returns to it much later in the volume. The answer is that the angular momentum was stored in the electromagnetic field all along, and was transferred to the disk when the field changed. The field momentum density is (ε₀E × B); the angular momentum is the integral of r × (that quantity).

The mechanism is that fields carry energy, momentum and angular momentum of their own, so conservation laws hold only when the fields are counted. The mechanical angular momentum of the disk is balanced by the field angular momentum that disappears when the current is switched off.

Implication: when a conservation law seems to fail, look for where the missing quantity is being stored. In electromagnetism the fields themselves are the storage medium.

## The principle of least action

One chapter, presented as a special lecture given almost verbatim, describes a different way of stating physical laws. Feynman recalls that his high-school physics teacher, Mr. Bader, introduced him to the principle of least action: the path a particle actually takes between two points is the one for which a certain quantity, the action, is smallest, or more precisely stationary.

From that single principle, Newton's laws of motion follow, and the principle extends to electromagnetism and, in Feynman's own research, to quantum mechanics. The Lagrangian formulation makes the connection to symmetries and conservation laws transparent.

The mechanism is that the same physics can be expressed as local rules governing each moment or as a principle governing the whole path, and each view reveals things the other hides. The least-action principle is global; the differential equations are local.

Implication: try stating a problem in more than one way, since a formulation that looks at the whole may reveal what step-by-step rules conceal. Least action is the formulation that most clearly connects classical and quantum mechanics.

## Matter, dielectrics and magnetism

The later chapters apply electromagnetism to matter: dielectrics, the internal geometry of crystals, the magnetism of materials including ferromagnetism, and elasticity. In a dielectric, an applied field polarizes the material; the polarization produces a bound charge that modifies the field inside. Feynman derives the relations between D, E and P and discusses the dielectric constant.

He treats paramagnetism, diamagnetism and ferromagnetism, including the domain structure of ferromagnetic materials and the hysteresis loop. Two chapters on fluids are titled "The Flow of Dry Water" and "The Flow of Wet Water," the first treating an idealized fluid without viscosity, the second the real fluids whose friction produces turbulence and drag. The volume ends with curved space, an introduction to the geometry of Einstein's general theory of relativity.

The mechanism is that the properties of everyday materials, their stiffness, magnetism and flow, arise from electromagnetic forces between atoms, described with the same tools developed for fields. Idealized models (perfect dielectrics, inviscid fluids) explain the leading behavior; the corrections (hysteresis, viscosity, turbulence) contain the interesting real-world effects.

Implication: treat idealized models as a first step. They explain much, but the gap between the ideal and the real is often where the interesting behavior lies. Ferromagnetism and turbulence are two such gaps.

## Limits

- It is demanding. It requires fluency in calculus and builds the mathematics of vector fields as it goes, so it suits readers who already know basic physics better than true beginners, as Feynman's own preface to the course acknowledged.
- It is not a problem book. It gives little practice in the calculations that physics courses require, and it works best alongside a conventional textbook and its exercises.
- Some of its arguments are contested. Its treatment of the vector potential and the Aharonov–Bohm effect has been criticized in detail by some physicists, though others defend it.
- The chapter on curved space is only an introduction; the full general theory of relativity is not developed.
