## Thesis

Electricity, magnetism and light are one phenomenon, described completely by Maxwell's equations, and the clearest way to understand them is through fields that fill space; the same mathematics then explains the behavior of matter, from dielectrics and magnets to elastic solids and flowing water, because nature uses the same patterns again and again.

## The main ideas

- Maxwell's discovery of the laws of electrodynamics was the most significant event of the nineteenth century.
- Electric and magnetic forces are best understood through fields, which carry energy and momentum of their own.
- The same equations have the same solutions, so a problem solved in one field of physics is solved in every field governed by those equations.
- The vector potential, once treated as a mathematical convenience, is physically real in quantum mechanics.
- Nature's laws can be stated as a principle of least action.
- The physics of matter, from magnets to fluids, follows from electromagnetism and the behavior of atoms.

## Maxwell's equations

Volume II of *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, published in 1964 and subtitled *Mainly Electromagnetism and Matter*, covers the second year of the course Richard Feynman taught at Caltech from 1961 to 1963, edited by Robert B. Leighton and Matthew Sands. Like the other volumes, it is free to read online at [The Feynman Lectures on Physics website](https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/).

Its first chapter makes a historical claim. From a long view of the history of mankind, seen from ten thousand years in the future, "the most significant event of the 19th century will be judged as Maxwell's discovery of the laws of electrodynamics." The American Civil War, Feynman adds, "will pale into provincial insignificance in comparison with this important scientific event of the same decade."

James Clerk Maxwell's equations, completed in the 1860s, united electricity, magnetism and light, predicted radio waves and became the foundation of electrical technology.

Implication: measure the importance of an event by its consequences over centuries, not by how much attention it drew at the time.

## Fields

Feynman builds the volume around the idea of the field: a quantity defined at every point in space, such as the electric field, which tells what force a charge would feel there. Rather than thinking of charges acting on each other at a distance, physics describes charges creating fields and fields acting on charges.

To work with fields, he first teaches the mathematics of vector calculus, the language of divergence, curl and flux in which Maxwell's equations are written, and then applies it to electrostatics and Gauss's law, which relates the field flowing out of a surface to the charge inside it.

The mechanism is that describing forces through fields turns a tangle of interactions between distant objects into local rules that hold at every point.

Implication: when relationships between distant parts of a system seem complicated, look for a description in terms of local quantities and local rules.

## The same equations, the same solutions

In a chapter on electrostatic analogs, Feynman points out that the equations of electrostatics also describe steady heat flow, the stretching of a membrane, the diffusion of neutrons and other phenomena. Since the same equations have the same solutions, a problem solved in one of these fields is solved in all of them.

He then asks why nature should use the same equations so often, and suggests that the answer lies less in a deep unity of substance than in common approximations: smoothed-out descriptions of space that lead naturally to the same mathematical forms.

The mechanism is that physical systems governed by the same mathematics behave alike, whatever they are made of.

Implication: when you face a new problem, ask whether its mathematical structure matches one that has already been solved, even in a very different field.

## The vector potential

Electromagnetism can be described with a quantity called the vector potential as well as with electric and magnetic fields. For most of the history of physics, the potential was treated as a mathematical convenience, with only the fields counting as real.

Feynman argues otherwise. Using a then recent prediction now known as the Aharonov–Bohm effect, he shows that in quantum mechanics electrons can be affected by the vector potential in regions where the magnetic field is zero. In quantum mechanics, he argues, the vector potential is a real field, and force becomes a secondary idea. Some physicists later questioned details of his account, and others have defended it.

The mechanism is that a quantity that seems redundant in one theory can turn out to be fundamental in a deeper one.

Implication: be cautious about dismissing parts of a description as mere bookkeeping. A deeper theory may show they matter.

## The disk paradox

Feynman poses a puzzle that has come to bear his name. A disk free to rotate carries electric charges around its rim and a coil of current-carrying wire at its center. When the current is switched off, the changing magnetic field produces an electric field that pushes the charges and sets the disk turning. But angular momentum must be conserved, and nothing seems to have supplied it.

He returns to it much later in the volume. The answer is that the angular momentum was stored in the electromagnetic field all along, and was transferred to the disk when the field changed.

The mechanism is that fields carry energy, momentum and angular momentum of their own, so conservation laws hold only when the fields are counted.

Implication: when a conservation law seems to fail, look for where the missing quantity is being stored.

## The principle of least action

One chapter, presented as a special lecture given almost verbatim, describes a different way of stating physical laws. Feynman recalls that his high-school physics teacher, Mr. Bader, introduced him to the principle of least action: the path a particle actually takes between two points is the one for which a certain quantity, the action, is smallest, or more precisely stationary.

From that single principle, Newton's laws of motion follow, and the principle extends to electromagnetism and, in Feynman's own research, to quantum mechanics.

The mechanism is that the same physics can be expressed as local rules governing each moment or as a principle governing the whole path, and each view reveals things the other hides.

Implication: try stating a problem in more than one way, since a formulation that looks at the whole may reveal what step-by-step rules conceal.

## Matter

The later chapters apply electromagnetism to matter: dielectrics, the internal geometry of crystals, the magnetism of materials including ferromagnetism, and elasticity. Two chapters on fluids are titled "The Flow of Dry Water" and "The Flow of Wet Water," the first treating an idealized fluid without viscosity, the second the real fluids whose friction produces turbulence and drag.

The volume ends with curved space, an introduction to the geometry of Einstein's general theory of relativity.

The mechanism is that the properties of everyday materials, their stiffness, magnetism and flow, arise from electromagnetic forces between atoms, described with the same tools developed for fields.

Implication: treat idealized models as a first step. They explain much, but the gap between the ideal and the real is often where the interesting behavior lies.

## Limits

- It is demanding. It requires fluency in calculus and builds the mathematics of vector fields as it goes, so it suits readers who already know basic physics better than true beginners, as Feynman's own preface to the course acknowledged.
- It is not a problem book. It gives little practice in the calculations that physics courses require, and it works best alongside a conventional textbook and its exercises.
- Some of its arguments are contested. Its treatment of the vector potential and the Aharonov–Bohm effect has been criticized in detail by some physicists, though others defend it.
