# A Different Universe
Robert B. Laughlin (2005)

Physical law is mostly emergent. The reliable behavior we observe at human scales is protected from the microscopic details underneath it.

Key concepts: emergence · protectorates · more is different · reductionism

## Overview

Philip Anderson's 1972 paper "More is Different" argued that the behavior of complex systems is not simply the sum of their parts. Laughlin, a condensed-matter physicist who shared the 1998 Nobel Prize for the fractional quantum Hall effect, takes the claim further. He treats emergence not as an occasional exception but as the normal source of the laws we use. The exactness of Newton's laws, the quantization of the quantum Hall effect, and the rigidity of solids are collective properties. They appear only when large numbers of particles organize themselves. Once organized, the higher-level law is insensitive to the precise microscopic rules.

**Emergence** is the appearance of stable, quantitative laws at a larger scale that cannot be derived by pure deduction from smaller-scale laws. Laughlin's concrete picture is the crystalline solid. Its low-energy vibrations (phonons) and its stiffness follow from the fact that the atoms have locked into a lattice. Measuring the sound speed tells you almost nothing about the detailed atomic potential. The lattice is a **protectorate**: a regime whose generic properties are fixed by the organizing principle and protected from the microscopic noise. Superfluid helium, the quantum Hall states, and ordinary metals are other examples. In each case the macroscopic law is exact within its regime even though the underlying particles are quantum and chaotic.

The implication is that the search for a single fundamental theory from which everything else follows is often looking in the wrong direction. Many of the laws that govern the world we inhabit are collective. They must be discovered by experiment at the scale where they operate. Attempts to derive them bottom-up from a more microscopic theory routinely fail because the higher law is protected. The same logic applies beyond physics. Biological organization, the flow of a city, and the existence of phases of matter all show stable patterns that are not transparent consequences of their constituents.

## Limits

Laughlin's examples from condensed matter are strong. His extensions to gravity, the vacuum, and biology are more speculative. Critics note that the book spends time on polemic against string theory and reductionist rhetoric that is not required for the central claim. The existence of protectorates does not imply that microscopic physics is irrelevant at every scale; it implies that some macroscopic regularities are robust against it.
