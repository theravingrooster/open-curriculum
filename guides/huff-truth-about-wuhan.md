## Thesis

The COVID-19 pandemic began with a virus made through gain-of-function research and released by accident from the Wuhan Institute of Virology, work that the US government paid for through EcoHealth Alliance, and the officials responsible then covered up the origin, making it, in Andrew Huff's account, the biggest lie in history.

## The main ideas

- SARS-CoV-2 was man-made through gain-of-function research and escaped from a Wuhan laboratory with inadequate biosafety.
- The US government is to blame for transferring dangerous biotechnology to China, paying for the work through grants to EcoHealth Alliance.
- Federal programs that funded virus hunting abroad were poorly overseen and, Huff argues, fraudulent.
- The research also served as cover for collecting intelligence on Chinese laboratories.
- Officials who knew the risks suppressed the lab-origin explanation once the pandemic began.
- Dangerous research should not be funded where it cannot be secured, and those responsible should be held to account.

## An insider's account

Andrew Huff worked at EcoHealth Alliance, a New York nonprofit that studied emerging diseases and collaborated with the Wuhan Institute of Virology, from 2014 to 2016. EcoHealth hired him as a senior research scientist and made him an associate vice president in 2015; he worked on computer models for assessing emerging disease threats. Before that, he writes, he served in the military and worked at Sandia National Laboratories with a security clearance, which shaped how he saw the organization.

The book, published in December 2022 with a foreword by Jason Bashura, a biodefense specialist, presents itself as an insider's warning. Its early chapters describe his career and his growing alarm at what he saw as weak security thinking at EcoHealth; the later ones lay out the origin of the pandemic and the cover-up that followed.

His vantage point is the inside of the organization that channeled US money to Wuhan. He did not work at the Wuhan laboratory himself and never traveled to China; his claim is to know how the funding and the organization worked.

Implication: when an institution's account is in question, the testimony of people who worked inside it is evidence worth weighing alongside official statements.

## A virus made in a laboratory

Huff's central claim is that SARS-CoV-2 was created through gain-of-function research, experiments that make viruses more transmissible or dangerous to study the risks they pose, and that it escaped from the Wuhan Institute of Virology because foreign laboratories lacked adequate controls for biosafety, biosecurity and risk management. In a chapter titled "COVID Made in America," he argues that the research behind it was American in its funding and design.

The US government has since adopted the central part of this conclusion. In April 2025 the White House replaced its COVID-19 website with a page titled "Lab Leak: The True Origins of COVID-19," which states that the virus has a biological characteristic not found in nature, that all cases stem from a single introduction into humans, and that Wuhan is home to China's foremost laboratory for research on such viruses, which had a history of gain-of-function research at inadequate biosafety levels. The FBI has assessed a laboratory origin as most likely, with moderate confidence; the Department of Energy and, in January 2025, the CIA reached the same conclusion with low confidence. A House select subcommittee concluded in December 2024 that the virus most likely emerged from a laboratory in Wuhan.

Implication: judge the origin question on the evidence of the laboratory's work and safety record, and on the government's own assessments, not on early official reassurances.

## Follow the money

Huff argues that the US government is to blame for the transfer of dangerous biotechnology to China. The money flowed through EcoHealth Alliance, which received federal grants, including one from the National Institutes of Health to study the risk of bat coronaviruses emerging in humans, and subcontracted part of the work to the Wuhan laboratory.

He devotes a chapter, "The $210 Million USAID PREDICT Fraud," to the US Agency for International Development's program for finding new viruses in animals abroad, in which EcoHealth was a major participant. In late 2021 he filed whistleblower allegations that EcoHealth had defrauded the government in its billing.

Federal action has since vindicated part of this account. In January 2025 the Department of Health and Human Services barred EcoHealth Alliance and its president, Peter Daszak, from federal funding for five years, finding that they had facilitated gain-of-function research in Wuhan without proper oversight and violated the terms of their NIH grant. The House select subcommittee found that EcoHealth had used taxpayer money for dangerous research in Wuhan and misled the NIH.

Implication: when research is paid for through layers of grants and subcontracts, follow the money to the place where the work is actually done, and ask who is overseeing it there.

## An intelligence cover

Huff's most distinctive argument is that the research in China served another purpose. He describes EcoHealth as an almost perfect cover for intelligence collection: by funding and collaborating with Chinese laboratories, the United States gained a view into their work and capabilities. He states that in late 2015 or early 2016 Daszak told him he was working with the CIA and asked whether he should continue.

This, in Huff's account, explains why the government protected the research and why the truth about the origin was so dangerous to those involved. He calls the pandemic the biggest US intelligence failure since September 11.

He also describes what he says followed his decision to speak out: harassment, surveillance and break-ins at his home, which he attributes to members of the intelligence community.

Implication: when a research program has security or intelligence value, expect that value to shape how its risks are judged and how its failures are disclosed.

## The cover-up

Huff's title claim is that the origin of the pandemic was covered up. Officials who had funded or approved the research, he argues, had every reason to promote a natural origin and to dismiss the possibility of a laboratory accident as a conspiracy theory.

Official findings since the book appeared support the core of this charge. The House select subcommittee concluded in December 2024 that "Proximal Origin," an influential 2020 scientific paper arguing for a natural origin, had been prompted by Anthony Fauci, then director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, to promote that narrative. In June 2026, the Director of National Intelligence, Tulsi Gabbard, released documents that her office said showed Fauci had funded gain-of-function research at the Wuhan laboratory, worked with officials in the intelligence community to suppress the lab-leak explanation and misled Congress.

Implication: when the institutions that would be blamed for a disaster control the investigation of its cause, treat their early conclusions with caution.

## Where we go from here

The book's final chapter, "Where Do We Go from Here?," turns from the origin of the pandemic to what should change, after a career Huff spent warning that dangerous research was being funded where it could not be properly secured.

The federal government has since moved in that direction. An executive order in May 2025 ended federal funding for dangerous gain-of-function research by foreign entities in countries of concern, including China, and ordered stricter oversight of such research in the United States; the NIH paused affected grants.

Implication: research whose failure could cause a pandemic should be funded only where it can be inspected and controlled, and the people who approve it should be answerable for the risks.

## Limits

- Several of its claims rest on Huff's testimony alone. His accounts of what Daszak told him and of surveillance and break-ins at his home have not been corroborated, and EcoHealth described his role as an associate vice president working on computer models.
- His inside view ends in 2016. He left EcoHealth more than three years before the outbreak, so his account of the research and events that followed relies on public records rather than his own observation.
