The Feynman Lectures on Physics

The full text of all three volumes is free to read online. This page turns the lectures into a usable self-study path.

Richard Feynman taught Caltech’s introductory physics course from 1961 to 1963. Robert B. Leighton and Matthew Sands edited the recorded lectures into three volumes. The New Millennium Edition, corrected and typeset for the web, is available at no charge from The Feynman Lectures on Physics website, run by Caltech with the editors Michael A. Gottlieb and Rudolf Pfeiffer. The site also holds the original lecture recordings, photographs of the blackboards, Feynman’s handwritten notes, and a problem-solving supplement.

The lectures are free to read in a browser. The posting does not transfer any right to download or redistribute the text. The Open Curriculum guides distill the central claims of each volume; the curriculum below sends you to the source.

The three volumes

  • Volume I — Mainly Mechanics, Radiation, and Heat (52 chapters). Atoms, Newton’s laws, conservation, relativity, oscillations, optics, and the first look at quantum behavior. Table of contents.
  • Volume II — Mainly Electromagnetism and Matter (42 chapters). Fields, Maxwell’s equations, matter, and a glimpse of curved space. Table of contents.
  • Volume III — Quantum Mechanics (21 chapters). Probability amplitudes, two-state systems, crystals, semiconductors, and superconductivity. Table of contents.

Full first-year path (Volume I)

For a sequential reading of mechanics, radiation, and heat. Skip the pure mathematics chapters on first pass if the algebra is heavy; return later.

  • Chapters 1–4: atoms, basic physics, relations to other sciences, energy.
  • Chapters 7–10: gravitation, motion, Newton’s laws, momentum.
  • Chapters 11–14: vectors, forces, work and potential energy.
  • Chapters 15–17: special relativity.
  • Chapters 18–21: rotation and the harmonic oscillator.
  • Chapters 26–34: optics, interference, diffraction, polarization.
  • Chapters 37–38: quantum behavior and wave-particle viewpoints.
  • Chapters 39–46: kinetic theory, statistical mechanics, thermodynamics.
  • Chapter 52: symmetry.

Start at I-1 and follow the next-chapter links on the official site.

Electromagnetism path (Volume II)

Requires comfort with vectors and some calculus. The early chapters build the mathematics of fields as they go.

  • Chapters 1–8: electromagnetism, vector calculus, electrostatics, Gauss’s law, energy.
  • Chapters 13–18: magnetostatics, the vector potential, induction, Maxwell’s equations.
  • Chapters 19–21: least action and solutions of Maxwell’s equations.
  • Chapters 34–39: magnetism of matter and elasticity.
  • Chapter 42: curved space.

Start at II-1.

Quantum path (Volume III)

Feynman begins with amplitudes and two-state systems rather than the Schrödinger equation. The logic is clearest in the simplest systems.

  • Chapters 1–4: quantum behavior, wave and particle viewpoints, probability amplitudes, identical particles.
  • Chapters 5–11: spin, the Hamiltonian, the ammonia maser, other two-state systems.
  • Chapters 13–14: propagation in a crystal lattice and semiconductors.
  • Chapter 19: the hydrogen atom and the periodic table.
  • Chapter 21: the Schrödinger equation in a classical context (superconductivity).

Start at III-1.

Supporting materials on the same site

  • Original lecture recordings — the unedited tapes of the 1961–63 course, with links that open the matching chapter.
  • Feynman’s Tips on Physics — four review lectures and selected exercises that fill the gap between ideas and problem-solving.
  • Exercises — problems drawn from the course and later collections, with solutions where available.
  • Original course handouts — the mimeographed notes, quizzes, and lab materials Feynman’s students actually received.

How the Open Curriculum guides fit

The three volume guides state each book’s central claim, the ideas that change decisions, the evidence Feynman used, and the limits of the argument. They are not substitutes for the lectures. Read a guide, then open the chapters it discusses.

  • Guide to Volume I
  • Guide to Volume II
  • Guide to Volume III
  • A Brief History of Time — a shorter popular tour of the largest scales, useful before or after the lectures.

The lectures are demanding. Feynman himself judged that he had not done well by most of his students. They work best for readers who already know some calculus and who treat them as a way of seeing rather than as a course in solving problems. The free official text, the recordings, and the Tips on Physics together form a complete free physics curriculum.
